Biological Classification and Origin
The potato is a starchy tuberous vegetable native to the Americas, specifically domesticated 7,000–10,000 years ago in the region of present-day southern Peru and extreme northwestern Bolivia. Biologically, it is the underground stem tuber of the plant Solanum tuberosum, a perennial herbaceous plant belonging to the nightshade family, Solanaceae. Despite growing underground, the tuber is technically a modified stem (specifically a thickened rhizome) rather than a root, evidenced by the presence of "eyes" which are vegetative buds arranged in a helical pattern. 

Physical Characteristics and Toxicity
Potato plants grow up to one meter high with hairy stems and leaves consisting of roughly four pairs of leaflets. The flowers vary in color from white and pink to blue or purple, with yellow centers. While the tubers are a global staple food, the aerial parts of the plant and the tubers themselves contain the toxin solanine. Properly grown and stored potatoes have negligible levels of this glycoalkaloid, but exposure to light can cause sprouts and skins to become toxic. After flowering, the plant produces small green fruits resembling cherry tomatoes, each containing approximately 300 seeds. 

Global Spread and Varieties
The Spanish introduced the potato to Europe in the second half of the 16th century during the Columbian Exchange. It has since become an integral part of the world's food supply, with over 5,000 different varieties existing today due to centuries of selective breeding. The most commonly cultivated species is S. tuberosum, which includes two major subspecies: the Andean potato (S. tuberosum andigena), adapted to short-day conditions, and the Chilean potato (S. tuberosum tuberosum), adapted to long-day conditions. Genetic studies indicate that over 99% of current potato varieties are direct descendants of a subspecies from south-central Chile.
