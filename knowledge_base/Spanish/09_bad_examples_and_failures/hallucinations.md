# Hallucinations

Hallucinations occur when AI models generate information that is factually incorrect, fabricated, or not grounded in reality. This is one of the most common and dangerous failure modes of large language models.

---

## What Are Hallucinations?

Hallucinations are confident-sounding but false statements generated by AI models. The model presents invented facts, citations, data, or events as if they were true.

**Example:**
> "The Treaty of Versailles was signed in 1925 by President Lincoln."

This statement is completely wrong:
- The Treaty of Versailles was signed in 1919, not 1925
- Abraham Lincoln was assassinated in 1865, decades before the treaty
- Woodrow Wilson was the US president during WWI

---

## Types of Hallucinations

### Factual Hallucinations
Making up facts about real-world entities, events, or data.

**Bad Example:**
> User: "When was Python 3.10 released?"
> Model: "Python 3.10 was released on March 15, 2022."
> 
> **Reality:** Python 3.10 was released on October 4, 2021.

### Citation Hallucinations
Inventing academic papers, books, URLs, or sources that don't exist.

**Bad Example:**
> User: "Find research on transformer attention mechanisms."
> Model: "See 'Attention Is Not All You Need' by Zhang et al., published in Nature Machine Intelligence, 2023."
> 
> **Reality:** This paper doesn't exist. The famous paper is "Attention Is All You Need" by Vaswani et al. (2017).

### Logical Hallucinations
Drawing incorrect conclusions from valid premises.

**Bad Example:**
> "All mammals are warm-blooded. Whales are mammals. Therefore, whales live on land."
> 
> The conclusion doesn't follow from the premises.

### Procedural Hallucinations
Describing steps or processes that won't work in practice.

**Bad Example:**
```python
# This code looks plausible but doesn't work
def download_file(url):
    response = requests.get(url)
    return response.download()  # No such method exists
```

---

## Why Hallucinations Happen

1. **Training Data Limitations**: Models learn patterns, not ground truth
2. **Probability-Based Generation**: Models predict likely tokens, not factual accuracy
3. **Lack of Verification**: No built-in fact-checking mechanism
4. **Overconfidence**: Models present uncertain information with high confidence
5. **Context Window Limits**: May forget or misremember earlier context

---

## How to Mitigate Hallucinations

### For Users

**Ask for Sources:**
> "Provide citations for any claims you make. If you're unsure, say so."

**Use Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG):**
> Ground responses in provided documents rather than training data.

**Request Uncertainty Acknowledgment:**
> "If you don't know something or aren't confident, explicitly state your uncertainty level."

**Verify Critical Information:**
> Always cross-check important facts, especially for medical, legal, or financial advice.

### For Developers

**Implement Fact-Checking Layers:**
```python
def verify_response(response, knowledge_base):
    claims = extract_claims(response)
    verified = []
    for claim in claims:
        if not knowledge_base.verify(claim):
            verified.append({"claim": claim, "verified": False})
        else:
            verified.append({"claim": claim, "verified": True})
    return verified
```

**Use Confidence Thresholds:**
```python
if response.confidence < 0.8:
    response += "\n[Note: This information may be uncertain]"
```

**Add Explicit Disclaimers:**
> System prompt: "You are a helpful assistant. When asked about facts you're uncertain about, say 'I'm not certain about this' rather than making up information."

---

## Red Flags for Hallucinations

Watch for these warning signs:

| Indicator | What It Suggests |
|-----------|------------------|
| Overly specific dates/names without sources | Possible fabrication |
| Perfect-sounding but unverifiable citations | Likely hallucinated |
| Contradictions within the same response | Model is confused |
| Claims that seem too convenient | May be invented |
| Technical details that don't match documentation | Probably wrong |

---

## Best Practices

1. **Never trust AI output blindly** for critical decisions
2. **Cross-reference** with authoritative sources
3. **Use AI for ideation**, not final facts
4. **Document verification steps** when using AI-generated content
5. **Build feedback loops** to catch and correct hallucinations
