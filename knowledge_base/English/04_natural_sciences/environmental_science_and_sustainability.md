# Environmental Science and Sustainability

Environmental science is what happens when you take the tools of physics, chemistry, biology, geology, and economics and point them at the question: "What is happening to our planet, and what can we do about it?" It is not a single discipline — it is a collision of disciplines, which is part of what makes it both fascinating and politically charged.

The core tension in environmental science is this: human civilization depends on extracting resources from the environment, but extracting those resources changes the environment in ways that can undermine the systems we depend on. Understanding that feedback loop is the entire field in a nutshell.

---

## Ecosystems — How Nature's Economy Works

An ecosystem is a network of relationships between living things and their physical surroundings. Energy flows in (mostly from the sun), gets passed through the food web, and eventually leaves as heat. Nutrients cycle through the system over and over.

### Energy Flow

The **10% rule** is the key insight: at each step in the food chain, roughly 90% of the energy is lost as heat. This is why there are far more plants than herbivores, and far more herbivores than predators. It is also why eating lower on the food chain is more energy-efficient.

```
Sunlight → Plants (producers) → Herbivores → Carnivores → Top predators
  100%          ~1%               ~0.1%      ~0.01%       ~0.001%
```

### Nutrient Cycles

Unlike energy, matter is recycled. The major biogeochemical cycles:

| Cycle | Key Process | Why It Matters |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| **Carbon** | Photosynthesis ↔ respiration ↔ decomposition | Regulates atmospheric CO₂; drives climate |
| **Nitrogen** | Bacterial fixation → nitrification → denitrification | Essential for proteins and DNA; limits plant growth |
| **Water** | Evaporation → condensation → precipitation → runoff | Distributes freshwater; drives weather |
| **Phosphorus** | Rock weathering → uptake by plants → decomposition | No atmospheric phase; often the limiting nutrient in ecosystems |

### Biodiversity and Resilience

Biodiversity is not just a nice-to-have — it is an insurance policy. Ecosystems with more species are more resilient to disturbance because if one species fails, others can fill its role. This is why the current extinction crisis matters. Species are disappearing at rates hundreds of times above the natural background rate. Scientists call it the sixth mass extinction — the first one caused by a single species (us).

Main threats to biodiversity, ranked by impact:

1. **Habitat destruction** — deforestation, urbanization, agriculture
2. **Climate change** — shifting temperature and precipitation patterns faster than species can adapt
3. **Overexploitation** — overfishing, hunting, harvesting faster than populations recover
4. **Pollution** — chemicals, plastics, excess nutrients (nitrogen/phosphorus runoff)
5. **Invasive species** — non-native organisms that outcompete or prey on native species

## Climate Science — The Big Picture

Climate and weather are not the same thing. Weather is what happens today. Climate is what typically happens over decades. A cold snap in January does not disprove climate change, just as a hot day in July does not prove it.

### The Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect is natural and necessary — without it, Earth's average temperature would be about −18°C instead of the current +15°C. The problem is that human activities are intensifying it.

| Greenhouse Gas | Primary Source | Atmospheric Lifetime | Relative Warming Potential |
|---------------|---------------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| **CO₂** | Fossil fuels, deforestation | 300-1,000 years | 1 (baseline) |
| **Methane (CH₄)** | Agriculture, natural gas leaks, wetlands | ~12 years | ~28-36× CO₂ |
| **N₂O** | Fertilizers, industrial processes | ~114 years | ~265-298× CO₂ |
| **Fluorinated gases** | Refrigerants, industrial | Hundreds to thousands of years | Thousands of times CO₂ |

CO₂ gets the most attention because it is the largest contributor by volume and persists for centuries. But methane is far more potent per molecule — which is why plugging natural gas leaks and managing agricultural emissions matter enormously.

### Evidence for Human-Caused Climate Change

- Global average temperature has risen ~1.1°C since pre-industrial times
- Arctic sea ice has declined ~40% in summer extent since 1979
- Sea levels have risen ~20 cm since 1900 (accelerating)
- Ocean pH has dropped 0.1 units (30% more acidic) since pre-industrial times
- Isotope analysis confirms the extra CO₂ comes from burning fossil fuels, not natural sources

### Mitigation vs. Adaptation

These are not either/or — both are necessary:

- **Mitigation** means reducing emissions: renewable energy, efficiency, carbon pricing, reforestation.
- **Adaptation** means adjusting to changes that are already locked in: sea walls, drought-resistant crops, managed retreat from flood zones, heat-resistant infrastructure.

## Renewable Energy — Powering the Transition

The energy transition is arguably the largest engineering project in human history. The goal: replace fossil fuels (which have powered civilization for 200 years) with clean energy sources, without crashing the economy or leaving billions without power.

| Source | How It Works | Capacity Factor | Main Challenge |
|--------|-------------|----------------|----------------|
| **Solar PV** | Photons knock electrons loose in silicon cells | 15-25% | Intermittent; no generation at night |
| **Wind** | Turbines convert kinetic energy of moving air | 25-45% | Intermittent; visual/noise concerns |
| **Hydroelectric** | Falling water spins turbines | 40-60% | Geography-limited; ecosystem disruption |
| **Geothermal** | Tap heat from Earth's interior | 70-90% | Location-limited (volcanic regions) |
| **Nuclear** | Fission heats water → steam → turbine | 85-93% | Cost, waste, public perception |

The intermittency problem (solar and wind are not always available) requires solutions: battery storage (lithium-ion, pumped hydro), grid interconnection (sunny regions send power to cloudy ones), demand response (shift usage to when supply is available), and baseload sources (nuclear, geothermal, hydro).

## Pollution — What We Put in the Air, Water, and Soil

### Air Pollution

Air pollution kills an estimated 7 million people per year (WHO). The main culprits:

- **Particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10)**: Tiny particles that penetrate deep into lungs and enter the bloodstream. From combustion, industry, and dust.
- **Ozone (O₃)**: Forms at ground level from reactions between nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds in sunlight. Damages lungs and crops.
- **NO₃ and SO₂**: From burning fossil fuels. Cause acid rain, respiratory disease, and water acidification.

### Water and Soil

- **Nutrient pollution**: Fertilizer runoff causes algal blooms that deplete oxygen, creating "dead zones" in lakes and coastal oceans. The Gulf of Mexico dead zone is roughly the size of Connecticut.
- **Plastic pollution**: About 8-10 million tons of plastic enter the ocean each year. It breaks down into microplastics, which have been found in drinking water, food, and human blood.
- **Emerging contaminants**: Pharmaceuticals, PFAS ("forever chemicals"), and microplastics are turning up in water supplies worldwide. Treatment technology is struggling to keep up.

## Water — The Resource We Cannot Replace

Only 2.5% of Earth's water is freshwater, and most of that is locked in ice. The freshwater we can actually use is less than 1% of all water on the planet.

| Sector | Share of Global Water Use |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Agriculture** | ~70% |
| **Industry** | ~20% |
| **Domestic** | ~10% |

Water scarcity already affects over 2 billion people. The problem is getting worse due to population growth, climate change (shifting precipitation patterns), and over-extraction of aquifers. The Ogallala Aquifer in the US Great Plains, which irrigates a huge amount of farmland, is being depleted faster than it recharges — and recharge would take centuries.

Solutions include drip irrigation (cuts water use by 30-60%), rainwater harvesting, wastewater recycling, desalination (energy-intensive but improving), and pricing water at its true value.

## Food Systems and Sustainability

About one-third of all food produced globally is wasted — roughly 1.3 billion tons per year. Meanwhile, about 800 million people face chronic hunger. This is not a production problem; it is a distribution and waste problem.

Sustainable agriculture practices that reduce environmental impact:

- **Crop rotation and polycultures**: Break pest cycles, improve soil health, reduce fertilizer needs
- **Reduced tillage**: Preserve soil structure, retain moisture, sequester carbon
- **Integrated pest management**: Use biological controls before chemical pesticides
- **Agroforestry**: Combine trees with crops for shade, soil protection, and biodiversity
- **Precision agriculture**: GPS and sensor data to apply water, fertilizer, and pesticides only where needed
- **Regenerative agriculture**: Focus on rebuilding soil health; can sequester significant carbon

## Environmental Policy — How Societies Respond

| Agreement / Policy | Year | What It Does |
|--------------------|------|-------------|
| **Montreal Protocol** | 1987 | Phased out CFCs; healing the ozone layer — often called the most successful environmental treaty |
| **Paris Agreement** | 2015 | Countries set voluntary emission reduction targets; goal of limiting warming to 1.5-2°C |
| **Carbon pricing** | Various | Carbon taxes or cap-and-trade systems; make pollution expensive |
| **Environmental impact assessment** | Various | Require analysis of environmental effects before major projects are approved |
| **Extended producer responsibility** | Various | Make manufacturers responsible for the full lifecycle of their products |

The precautionary principle — act to prevent harm even when scientific certainty is incomplete — is controversial. Industry argues it can stifle innovation. Environmental scientists counter that waiting for certainty on issues like climate change means waiting until it is too late to act.

## Summary

Environmental science is fundamentally about trade-offs. Every choice has consequences — for the economy, for ecosystems, for future generations. The challenge is not choosing between "the environment" and "the economy" — it is figuring out how to build a civilization that can thrive within the physical limits of a finite planet. The good news is that we have the knowledge and technology to make better choices. The harder part is the politics, economics, and human behavior that determine whether we actually use them.
