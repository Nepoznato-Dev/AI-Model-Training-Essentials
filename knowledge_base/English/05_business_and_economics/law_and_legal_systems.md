# Law and Legal Systems

Law is the set of rules that a society agrees to enforce through its institutions. It is not the same as morality — you can do something legal that is immoral, and something illegal that is morally justified. Law exists to provide predictability: to establish what is allowed, what is forbidden, and what happens when the rules are broken. Without it, disputes are settled by power. With it, disputes are settled by process.

This file covers the major legal systems, the main branches of law, and the concepts that shape how legal systems work in practice.

---

## Legal Systems of the World

Not all legal systems work the same way. The three dominant traditions:

| System | Basis | Where Used | How Judges Decide |
|--------|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Common law** | Judicial precedent (past court decisions) | UK, US, Canada, Australia, India | Judges interpret statutes and apply precedent from similar past cases |
| **Civil law** | Comprehensive legal codes | Most of Europe, Latin America, Japan, South Korea | Judges apply the written code to the case; precedent is less binding |
| **Religious law** | Religious texts and interpretation | Islamic countries (Sharia), Israel (rabbinical courts for some matters) | Religious authorities interpret sacred texts |

**Common law** is judge-heavy: when no statute covers a situation, judges look at how similar cases were decided in the past (precedent / *stare decisis*). This means common law evolves through court decisions — judges effectively make law through interpretation.

**Civil law** is code-heavy: comprehensive legal codes cover most situations, and judges apply the code. Past decisions are informative but not binding. This system is more predictable but less flexible.

Most countries use a mix. The US, for example, has common law at its core but civil law in Louisiana (influenced by French and Spanish legal traditions).

## Branches of Law

### Public Law vs. Private Law

**Public law** governs the relationship between individuals and the state:

- **Constitutional law**: The rules about how government is structured and what powers it has. Constitutional courts can strike down laws that violate the constitution (judicial review).
- **Criminal law**: The state prosecutes individuals for offenses against society. The burden of proof is "beyond a reasonable doubt" — the highest standard in law.
- **Administrative law**: Challenges to government agency decisions. If a regulator denies your permit or a tax authority assesses you unfairly, administrative law is how you challenge it.

**Private law** (also called civil law, confusingly) governs relationships between individuals:

- **Contract law**: Enforceable agreements between parties
- **Tort law**: Civil wrongs — when someone's negligence or intentional act harms you
- **Property law**: Who owns what, and what rights come with ownership
- **Family law**: Marriage, divorce, child custody, adoption

### Criminal vs. Civil Cases

| Feature | Criminal Case | Civil Case |
|---------|--------------|------------|
| **Who brings it** | The state (prosecution) | The injured party (plaintiff) |
| **Burden of proof** | Beyond a reasonable doubt | Preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not) |
| **Outcome** | Punishment (prison, fine, probation) | Compensation (damages) or court order |
| **Jury** | Usually yes | Sometimes |
| **Example** | Theft, assault, murder | Breach of contract, personal injury, property dispute |

The same act can lead to both criminal and civil cases. O.J. Simpson was acquitted in criminal court (the state did not prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt) but found liable in civil court (the plaintiff proved it was more likely than not that he was responsible).

## Constitutional Law

A constitution is the supreme law of a country. It establishes the structure of government, distributes powers among branches, and protects fundamental rights. Everything else — statutes, regulations, executive orders — must be consistent with it.

### Key Principles

- **Separation of powers**: Government is divided into legislative (makes laws), executive (enforces laws), and judicial (interprets laws) branches. Each branch checks the others.
- **Judicial review**: Courts can declare laws or government actions unconstitutional. In the US, the Supreme Court established this power in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803).
- **Fundamental rights**: Most constitutions protect core freedoms — speech, religion, assembly, due process, equal protection. These rights are not absolute; the government can restrict them if it has a compelling reason and uses the least restrictive means.
- **Federalism** (in federal systems): Power is divided between national and state/provincial governments. Some powers belong exclusively to one level; others are shared.

## Contract Law

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement. Not every promise is a contract — for a contract to be valid, it needs specific elements.

### Elements of a Valid Contract

| Element | What It Means | Example |
|---------|---------------|---------|
| **Offer** | A clear proposal with specific terms | "I will sell you my car for $5,000" |
| **Acceptance** | Agreement to those exact terms | "I accept your offer to buy the car for $5,000" |
| **Consideration** | Something of value exchanged by both parties | Money from buyer; car from seller |
| **Capacity** | Legal ability to enter a contract | Minors and mentally incapacitated persons may lack capacity |
| **Legality** | The contract's purpose must be legal | A contract to commit a crime is void |

### When Contracts Go Wrong

**Breach of contract** occurs when one party fails to perform their obligations. Remedies include:

- **Damages**: Money to compensate for the loss (most common)
- **Specific performance**: Court orders the breaching party to fulfill the contract (rare — usually only for unique items like real estate)
- **Rescission**: Contract is canceled; both parties return to their pre-contract position

Defenses to breach include: the contract was based on fraud, one party lacked capacity, performance became impossible (force majeure), or the terms were unconscionable.

## Tort Law — When Someone Gets Hurt

Tort law addresses civil wrongs that cause harm. Unlike criminal law (where the state prosecutes), tort law lets injured parties sue for compensation.

### Types of Torts

| Type | What It Requires | Example |
|------|-----------------|---------|
| **Negligence** | Duty of care → breach → causation → damages | A driver runs a red light and hits your car |
| **Intentional torts** | Deliberate harmful action | Assault, defamation, trespass |
| **Strict liability** | Liability regardless of fault | Defective products, dangerous activities, dog bites (in some states) |

**Negligence** is by far the most common tort. To win a negligence case, the plaintiff must prove four things:

1. The defendant had a **duty of care** (drivers have a duty to drive safely)
2. The defendant **breached** that duty (they ran a red light)
3. The breach **caused** the harm (the collision would not have happened otherwise)
4. The plaintiff suffered actual **damages** (medical bills, lost wages, pain)

## Property Law

Property law defines who owns what and what rights come with ownership.

### Types of Property

| Type | Examples | Key Rules |
|------|---------|-----------|
| **Real property** | Land, buildings, fixtures | Ownership transferred by deed; recorded in public records |
| **Personal property** | Cars, furniture, equipment | Ownership transferred by possession or bill of sale |
| **Intellectual property** | Patents, copyrights, trademarks | Intangible; protected by registration or use |

**Intellectual property** deserves special attention because it is increasingly important in the digital economy:

- **Patents** (20 years): Protect inventions. You get a monopoly on making, using, or selling the invention in exchange for publicly disclosing how it works.
- **Copyrights** (life + 70 years): Protect original creative works — books, music, software, art. You do not need to register; copyright exists from the moment of creation.
- **Trademarks** (renewable indefinitely): Protect brand identifiers — names, logos, slogans. Prevents consumer confusion.
- **Trade secrets**: Protect confidential business information (the Coca-Cola recipe, Google's search algorithm). No registration needed, but protection ends if the secret is independently discovered.

## Employment Law

Employment law governs the relationship between employers and employees. It varies significantly by country and (in the US) by state.

### Key Concepts

| Concept | What It Means | Varies By |
|---------|---------------|-----------|
| **At-will employment** | Either party can terminate at any time, for any legal reason | US (most states); most other countries require cause |
| **Minimum wage** | Lowest legal hourly pay | Country, state, city |
| **Anti-discrimination** | Cannot fire/refuse to hire based on race, gender, age, religion, disability | Protected categories vary by jurisdiction |
| **Workers' compensation** | Benefits for work-related injuries (no fault required) | State-level in the US |
| **Non-compete agreements** | Restrict where an employee can work after leaving | Increasingly restricted; banned in some US states |
| **Overtime** | Extra pay for hours beyond standard workweek | US: 1.5x after 40 hours/week |

The trend in many countries is toward more worker protection: mandatory paid leave, pay transparency laws, restrictions on non-competes, and classification of gig workers. These changes directly affect how businesses operate.

## The Legal Process

### How a Civil Lawsuit Works

1. **Filing**: Plaintiff files a complaint stating the legal claims and requested relief.
2. **Response**: Defendant answers (admits/denies allegations) or files a motion to dismiss.
3. **Discovery**: Both sides exchange information — documents, depositions, interrogatories. This is where most cases are won or lost.
4. **Pre-trial motions**: Either side may ask the judge to rule on specific issues or dismiss the case entirely (summary judgment).
5. **Trial**: Both sides present evidence and arguments to a judge or jury.
6. **Verdict and judgment**: The decision is rendered.
7. **Appeal**: The losing party can challenge the decision in a higher court, arguing legal errors.

Most civil cases never reach trial — roughly 95-97% settle before trial, often during or after discovery when both sides have a clearer picture of the evidence.

### Alternative Dispute Resolution

| Method | How It Works | Binding? | Cost |
|--------|-------------|----------|------|
| **Negotiation** | Parties talk directly (or through lawyers) | No | Low |
| **Mediation** | Neutral third party facilitates discussion | No | Medium |
| **Arbitration** | Neutral third party hears both sides and decides | Usually yes | Medium-High |
| **Litigation** | Court process with judge and possibly jury | Yes | High |

Many contracts include mandatory arbitration clauses, which require disputes to be resolved through arbitration rather than court. These are controversial — they are generally faster and cheaper, but they limit access to the court system and the results are typically not public.

## International Law

International law governs relations between countries. It is fundamentally different from domestic law because there is no world government to enforce it. Compliance depends on mutual interest, reciprocity, and international pressure.

| Area | What It Covers | Enforcement |
|------|---------------|-------------|
| **Treaties** | Binding agreements between countries | Mutual consent; withdrawal possible |
| **International humanitarian law** | Rules of war; protection of civilians | International Criminal Court, ad hoc tribunals |
| **Human rights law** | Universal rights inherent to all people | UN mechanisms, regional courts, domestic implementation |
| **Trade law (WTO)** | Rules governing international commerce | WTO dispute settlement; trade sanctions |
| **Law of the sea** | Maritime boundaries, navigation, resources | International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea |
| **Private international law** | Which country's law applies in cross-border disputes | Complex; depends on jurisdiction rules |

## Summary

Law is not just for lawyers. Understanding the basics — how contracts work, what your rights are, how the legal system processes disputes — is essential for anyone running a business, signing an agreement, or participating in civic life. The legal system is imperfect: it is expensive, slow, and sometimes produces unjust results. But it exists to replace violence and arbitrary power with process and predictability. The more you understand about how it works, the better you can navigate it — and the better you can advocate for changes when it falls short.
