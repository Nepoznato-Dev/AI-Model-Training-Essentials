# Behavioural Economics and Decision Science

Behavioural economics studies how people actually make decisions — not how rational agents would make them. Traditional economics assumes people are rational, self-interested, and good at processing information. Decades of research by Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler, and others have shown this is wildly inaccurate. People use mental shortcuts, are influenced by irrelevant context, overweight recent information, and make systematically predictable errors. Understanding these patterns is essential for designing better products, policies, organisations, and personal decisions.

---

## Two Systems of Thinking

| System | Characteristics | Speed | Effort | Example |
|--------|----------------|-------|--------|---------|
| **System 1** | Automatic; intuitive; emotional | Fast | Low | Reading facial expressions; driving a familiar route |
| **System 2** | Deliberate; analytical; logical | Slow | High | Solving a math problem; comparing product specifications |

**Key insight**: most decisions are made by System 1. System 2 is lazy and only engages when System 1 is stumped. This means most of our decisions are intuitive, not rational — and we construct rationalisations after the fact.

## Cognitive Biases

### Judgement Biases

| Bias | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Anchoring** | Over-relying on the first piece of information encountered | Salary negotiations: the first number mentioned anchors the discussion |
| **Availability heuristic** | Judging probability by how easily examples come to mind | Overestimating plane crash risk because crashes are widely reported |
| **Representativeness** | Judging probability by similarity to a stereotype | Assuming a quiet person is a librarian rather than a farmer (ignoring base rates) |
| **Framing effect** | Decisions change based on how options are presented | "90% survival rate" feels better than "10% mortality rate" |
| **Hindsight bias** | "I knew it all along" — seeing past events as predictable | After a market crash, people claim it was obvious |
| **Dunning-Kruger effect** | Low-ability people overestimate their ability; experts underestimate theirs | Novice investors confident they can beat the market |

### Decision Biases

| Bias | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Loss aversion** | Losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good | Holding losing stocks too long; avoiding necessary risks |
| **Status quo bias** | Preferring the current state of affairs | Not switching to a better health plan or pension fund |
| **Endowment effect** | Overvaluing what you already own | Asking more to sell a mug than you'd pay to buy it |
| **Sunk cost fallacy** | Continuing because of past investment, not future value | Finishing a bad book because you've already read half |
| **Confirmation bias** | Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs | Only reading news sources that agree with your views |
| **Present bias (hyperbolic discounting)** | Overweighting immediate rewards vs future ones | Procrastinating; undersaving for retirement |
| **Bandwagon effect** | Doing something because others are doing it | Buying assets during a bubble |
| **Decoy effect** | Adding an inferior option to make another option look better | Three pricing tiers where the middle option exists to make the top look reasonable |

## Heuristics

| Heuristic | Description | When It Helps | When It Fails |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|---------------|
| **Recognition** | Choose what you recognise | Quick decisions in familiar domains | Unfamiliar but better options are overlooked |
| **Take-the-best** | Choose based on the most important cue | When one factor dominates | When multiple factors matter equally |
| **Satisficing** | Choose the first option that meets minimum criteria | When search costs are high | Settling for "good enough" when better exists |
| **Elimination by aspects** | Eliminate options one attribute at a time | When comparing many options | Order of attributes changes the outcome |
| **Affect heuristic** | Make decisions based on emotions | Quick threat assessment | Emotional responses override data |

## Prospect Theory

Kahneman and Tversky's alternative to expected utility theory:

| Principle | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Reference dependence** | People evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point (usually the status quo), not in absolute terms |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses loom roughly twice as large as equivalent gains |
| **Diminishing sensitivity** | The difference between $100 and $200 feels larger than between $1,100 and $1,200 |
| **Probability weighting** | People overweight small probabilities and underweight large ones |

### The Value Function

| Region | Shape | Behaviour |
|--------|-------|-----------|
| **Gains** | Concave (risk-averse) | Prefer a sure $500 over 50% chance of $1,000 |
| **Losses** | Convex (risk-seeking) | Prefer 50% chance of losing $1,000 over a sure loss of $500 |
| **Loss domain is steeper** | Loss aversion | Losing $500 hurts more than gaining $500 feels good |

## Nudge Theory

### Principles of Choice Architecture

| Principle | Description | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Default options** | People tend to accept defaults | Opt-out organ donation dramatically increases donation rates |
| **Simplification** | Reduce complexity; make it easy | Auto-enrolment in pension plans |
| **Social proof** | People follow what others do | "9 out of 10 guests reuse their towels" |
| **Salience** | Make important information prominent | Calorie counts on menus; energy labels on appliances |
| **Commitment devices** | Lock in future behaviour | Automatic savings transfers; pre-commitment to exercise |
| **Timing** | When you ask matters | Enrol in a pension on payday, not at month end |

### Nudge vs Sludge

| Concept | Description | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **Nudge** | Making it easier to do the right thing | Default enrolment; simplified forms |
| **Sludge** | Excessive friction that prevents good decisions | Bureaucratic forms; hidden cancellation processes |
| **Sludge audit** | Identifying and removing unnecessary friction | Counting how many clicks/steps to complete a task |

## Applications

### In Business

| Application | Behavioural Insight | Result |
|-------------|-------------------|--------|
| **Pricing** | Anchoring; decoy effect | Three-tier pricing; premium option boosts mid-tier sales |
| **Marketing** | Scarcity; social proof | "Only 2 left in stock"; "Best seller" labels |
| **Product design** | Default options; friction | Pre-selected options; one-click purchasing |
| **Negotiation** | Framing; anchoring | First offer anchors the negotiation |
| **Employee behaviour** | Social norms; commitment | Public goals; peer recognition |

### In Public Policy

| Application | Behavioural Insight | Example |
|-------------|-------------------|---------|
| **Tax compliance** | Social proof | "9 out of 10 people in your area pay their taxes on time" |
| **Health** | Defaults; commitment | Opt-out organ donation; automatic appointment scheduling |
| **Savings** | Present bias; defaults | Auto-enrolment in retirement plans; save-more-tomorrow |
| **Energy** | Social comparison; salience | Comparing energy use with neighbours |
| **Education** | Simplification; nudges | Simplified FAFSA forms; text message reminders |

## Limitations and Criticisms

| Criticism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Replication crisis** | Some classic findings (e.g., ego depletion) have failed to replicate |
| **Effect sizes** | Some nudges have small effects that may not justify implementation costs |
| **Ethical concerns** | Who decides what the "right" choice is? Paternalism vs autonomy |
| **Context dependence** | Biases are stronger in some contexts than others; lab findings may not generalise |
| **Oversimplification** | Human behaviour is complex; biases don't explain everything |
| **Backfire effects** | Some nudges can trigger reactance or reduce intrinsic motivation |

## Summary

Behavioural economics reveals that human decision-making is systematically different from the rational agent model. System 1 (fast, intuitive) dominates System 2 (slow, analytical). Cognitive biases — anchoring, availability, loss aversion, status quo bias — are predictable and pervasive. Prospect theory shows that people evaluate outcomes relative to reference points, are loss-averse, and have diminishing sensitivity to changes. Nudge theory uses these insights to design choice architectures that help people make better decisions without restricting freedom. Applications span business (pricing, marketing, product design) and public policy (tax compliance, savings, health). But the field has limitations: replication failures, small effect sizes, and ethical questions about paternalism. The practical takeaway is to design systems that account for how people actually behave, not how we wish they would.
