# Business and Economics

Every organization that sells a product or service is a business, and every society that produces and distributes goods is an economy. The concepts in this file apply whether you are running a freelance operation, working at a corporation, trying to understand the news, or just figuring out how money moves through the world.

---

## How Businesses Are Structured

The legal structure of a business determines how it pays taxes, who is liable for debts, and how decisions are made.

| Structure | Owners | Liability | Tax Treatment | Best For |
|-----------|--------|-----------|---------------|----------|
| **Sole proprietorship** | 1 person | Unlimited personal liability | Income reported on personal return | Freelancers, side businesses |
| **Partnership** | 2+ people | Unlimited personal liability (general partners) | Income split among partners | Professional services (law firms, consultants) |
| **LLC** | 1+ members | Limited liability (personal assets protected) | Flexible — can be taxed as sole prop, partnership, or corporation | Most small-to-medium businesses |
| **C-Corporation** | Shareholders | Limited liability | Double taxation (corporate + dividend tax) | Companies planning to raise venture capital or go public |
| **S-Corporation** | Shareholders (limited to 100) | Limited liability | Pass-through (no double taxation) | Small corporations wanting tax benefits |

### Corporate Hierarchy

Most medium-to-large companies follow a similar structure:

- **Board of Directors**: Elected by shareholders; sets strategy and hires the CEO.
- **C-Suite**: CEO (overall strategy), CFO (finances), COO (operations), CTO (technology).
- **VPs / Directors**: Lead departments or business units.
- **Managers**: Oversee teams and day-to-day execution.
- **Individual contributors**: The people doing the actual work.

This hierarchy exists in almost every large organization, though titles and reporting structures vary.

## Economics — How Societies Allocate Resources

Economics studies how scarce resources are distributed among unlimited wants. It is divided into two branches:

**Microeconomics** looks at individual decisions: How does a consumer choose between products? How does a company set its price? What happens when a new competitor enters the market?

**Macroeconomics** looks at the big picture: Why do some countries grow while others stagnate? What causes recessions? How does inflation affect purchasing power?

### Supply and Demand — The Core Mechanism

Almost everything in economics flows from supply and demand. When demand for something increases (more people want it), prices rise. When supply increases (more of it is available), prices fall. The point where supply meets demand is the **equilibrium price**.

| Concept | What Happens | Real-World Example |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Demand increases, supply constant** | Prices rise | Housing in popular cities |
| **Supply increases, demand constant** | Prices fall | Electronics as technology improves |
| **Both increase equally** | Quantity rises, price stays roughly stable | Consumer goods over time |
| **Supply shock** | Prices spike suddenly | Oil crisis, chip shortage |
| **Demand shock** | Prices crash suddenly | Pandemic travel collapse |

### Key Economic Indicators

These numbers tell you whether an economy is healthy, overheating, or in trouble:

| Indicator | What It Measures | Why It Matters |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| **GDP** | Total value of goods and services produced | The size and growth rate of the economy |
| **GDP growth rate** | How fast the economy is expanding | >2-3% is healthy; negative = recession |
| **Inflation (CPI)** | Rate of price increases | Moderate (2%) is healthy; high erodes savings |
| **Unemployment rate** | % of workforce without jobs | High unemployment = wasted resources and human suffering |
| **Interest rates** | Cost of borrowing money | Set by central banks; low rates stimulate, high rates cool down |
| **Trade balance** | Exports minus imports | Persistent deficits can signal structural problems |
| **Consumer confidence** | How optimistic people feel about the economy | Drives spending, which is ~70% of GDP in developed countries |

### The Business Cycle

Economies do not grow in a straight line. They cycle through phases:

1. **Expansion**: Growth, low unemployment, rising wages, increasing confidence
2. **Peak**: Maximum growth; inflation may start rising; assets may be overvalued
3. **Contraction (Recession)**: Declining output, rising unemployment, falling confidence
4. **Trough**: The bottom; recovery begins as prices adjust and demand returns

Recessions are typically defined as two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth. They are caused by various triggers — financial crises, oil shocks, pandemic disruptions, bursting asset bubbles — but the pattern is always similar: confidence drops, spending falls, businesses cut jobs, which reduces spending further.

Central banks (like the US Federal Reserve) try to smooth the cycle by adjusting interest rates: lower rates during recessions to encourage borrowing and spending, higher rates during booms to prevent overheating.

## Marketing — How Companies Reach Customers

Marketing is not just advertising. It is the entire process of understanding what customers need, creating products that meet those needs, pricing them appropriately, distributing them where customers can buy them, and communicating their value.

### The Marketing Mix (4 Ps)

| P | What It Means | Key Questions |
|---|---------------|---------------|
| **Product** | What you sell | What problem does it solve? What makes it different? |
| **Price** | What you charge | What will customers pay? What do competitors charge? |
| **Place** | Where you sell it | Online? Retail? Direct-to-consumer? |
| **Promotion** | How you tell people | Advertising, social media, PR, content marketing? |

### Key Marketing Metrics

- **CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)**: How much it costs to get one new customer. If you spend $10,000 on ads and get 100 customers, your CAC is $100.
- **CLV (Customer Lifetime Value)**: How much revenue one customer generates over their entire relationship with your company. If the average customer spends $50/month for 3 years, CLV = $1,800.
- **The CAC:CLV ratio** should be at least 1:3 — it should cost at most a third of what the customer will pay you. If CAC exceeds CLV, the business loses money on every customer.
- **Conversion rate**: What percentage of people who see your offer actually buy. Typical e-commerce conversion rates are 1-3%.
- **Churn rate**: What percentage of customers leave each period. A 5% monthly churn means you lose half your customers in about 14 months.

## Accounting — The Language of Business

Accounting is how businesses record, report, and analyze financial information. Every business decision has a financial dimension, and accounting is the tool for understanding it.

### The Fundamental Equation

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

This equation always balances. Assets are what the business owns (cash, equipment, inventory). Liabilities are what it owes (loans, accounts payable). Equity is what is left for the owners.

### The Three Financial Statements

| Statement | What It Shows | Time Frame | Key Question It Answers |
|-----------|--------------|------------|------------------------|
| **Income Statement** (P&L) | Revenue, expenses, profit | Period (quarter, year) | "Did we make money?" |
| **Balance Sheet** | Assets, liabilities, equity | Point in time | "What is the company worth?" |
| **Cash Flow Statement** | Cash in, cash out | Period | "Do we have enough cash to operate?" |

A company can be profitable on the income statement but still run out of cash (if customers have not paid yet, or if it spent heavily on equipment). This is why the cash flow statement matters — cash is what keeps the lights on.

### Key Financial Ratios

| Ratio | Formula | What It Tells You |
|-------|---------|-------------------|
| **Gross margin** | (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue | How much profit on each dollar of sales before overhead |
| **Net margin** | Net income / Revenue | How much profit on each dollar after everything |
| **Current ratio** | Current assets / Current liabilities | Can the company pay its short-term bills? |
| **Debt-to-equity** | Total debt / Equity | How leveraged is the company? |
| **ROI** | Gain / Cost | Was the investment worth it? |

## Entrepreneurship and Startups

A startup is a temporary organization searching for a scalable business model. That definition (from Steve Blank) captures the key difference between a startup and a small business: a startup is looking for something new, while a small business executes something proven.

### The Startup Lifecycle

1. **Ideation**: Identify a problem worth solving. Talk to potential customers.
2. **Validation**: Build a minimum viable product (MVP) — the simplest version that tests your core assumptions.
3. **Product-market fit**: Iterate until customers are pulling the product out of your hands. This is the single most important milestone.
4. **Scaling**: Once you have product-market fit, invest in growth — hiring, marketing, expanding.
5. **Maturity / Exit**: The company stabilizes, goes public (IPO), or gets acquired.

### Funding Stages

| Stage | Typical Amount | Source | What It Funds |
|-------|---------------|--------|---------------|
| **Bootstrapping** | Personal savings | Founder | Initial development, proving the concept |
| **Pre-seed** | $50K-$500K | Friends, family, angel investors | MVP, early team |
| **Seed** | $500K-$5M | Angel investors, early-stage VCs | Product development, initial customers |
| **Series A** | $5M-$20M | Venture capital | Scaling product and go-to-market |
| **Series B+** | $20M+ | Venture capital, growth equity | Rapid growth, new markets |
| **IPO** | $100M+ | Public markets | Expansion, acquisitions, liquidity for early investors |

Most startups fail. Estimates suggest 90% of venture-backed startups do not return the initial investment. The most common reasons: no market need (42%), ran out of cash (29%), wrong team (23%), and being outcompeted (19%).

## Supply Chain and Operations

Supply chain management is the coordinated process of delivering the right product to the right place at the right time at the right cost. The objective is straightforward; the execution is complex.

### Key Concepts

- **Just-in-time (JIT)**: Receive materials only when needed, reducing inventory costs. Toyota pioneered this. The trade-off: JIT systems are fragile when supply chains are disrupted (as the 2020-2022 chip shortage demonstrated).
- **Lean manufacturing**: Eliminate waste — anything that does not add value to the customer. Identify and remove the "seven wastes": overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, and defects.
- **Six Sigma**: Reduce defects to 3.4 per million opportunities. Uses data and statistical methods to identify and eliminate sources of variation.
- **Bullwhip effect**: Small changes in consumer demand cause increasingly large swings upstream in the supply chain. A 5% increase in retail sales can translate to a 40% swing in manufacturer orders.

## International Trade

Countries trade because they have different strengths. David Ricardo's concept of **comparative advantage** (1817) remains the foundation: even if Country A is better at producing everything than Country B, both benefit if A focuses on what it is most efficient at and B focuses on what it is least bad at.

| Concept | What It Means | Example |
|---------|---------------|---------|
| **Tariffs** | Taxes on imported goods | US tariffs on Chinese goods; protects domestic industry but raises prices |
| **Free trade agreements** | Reduce barriers between countries | USMCA (US-Mexico-Canada), EU single market |
| **Trade deficit** | Imports > exports | US runs persistent trade deficits |
| **Trade surplus** | Exports > imports | China, Germany run persistent surpluses |
| **Currency exchange rates** | Value of one currency relative to another | Weak dollar makes exports cheaper, imports more expensive |

Globalization has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty (especially in China and India) but has also displaced workers in developed countries whose jobs moved overseas. The political tension between the benefits of trade and its distributional consequences is one of the defining issues of modern economics.

## Business Ethics and Corporate Responsibility

Businesses exist within societies, and their decisions affect people beyond their shareholders. The question is not whether businesses should be ethical — it is how to balance profit with responsibility.

| Framework | Focus | Example |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| **CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility)** | Voluntary commitment to social and environmental goals | Patagonia's environmental activism; TOMS "one for one" model |
| **ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance)** | Measurable criteria for evaluating company behavior | Carbon emissions, board diversity, labor practices |
| **B-Corp certification** | Legal requirement to consider stakeholders, not just shareholders | Ben & Jerry's, Danone |
| **Stakeholder capitalism** | Serve customers, employees, communities, and shareholders | Contrast with "shareholder primacy" (profits above all) |

The debate between shareholder primacy (Milton Friedman: "the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits") and stakeholder capitalism (businesses should serve all stakeholders) is far from settled. Evidence increasingly suggests that companies treating employees well, operating sustainably, and maintaining ethical standards tend to outperform over the long term — but the short-term incentives often push in the other direction.

## Summary

Business and economics are not separate from daily life — they are the infrastructure of it. Understanding supply and demand helps you make sense of prices. Understanding accounting helps you evaluate whether a business is healthy. Understanding marketing helps you see why you buy what you buy. Understanding corporate structures helps you navigate organizations. And understanding economics helps you make sense of the world — why some countries are rich and others poor, why recessions happen, and what policies might fix them. You do not need an MBA to benefit from these concepts. You just need to understand the basics.
