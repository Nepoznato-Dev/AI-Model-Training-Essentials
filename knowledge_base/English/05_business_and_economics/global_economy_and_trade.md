# Global Economy and Trade

The global economy is the largest and most complex system humans have ever built. In 2025, global GDP exceeded $110 trillion. Over 8 billion people produce, trade, and consume goods and services across borders every day. Understanding how this system works — who trades what, why some countries are rich and others poor, how money moves across borders, and what happens when the system breaks — is essential for navigating the modern world.

---

## Economic Indicators

The health of an economy is measured through several key indicators. Each tells a different part of the story.

| Indicator | What It Measures | Why It Matters |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| **GDP** (Gross Domestic Product) | Total value of goods and services produced in a country per year | Overall economic size and growth; the most cited measure |
| **GDP per capita** | GDP divided by population | Rough measure of average living standards |
| **GDP growth rate** | Year-over-year change in GDP | Whether the economy is expanding or contracting |
| **Inflation rate (CPI)** | Rate at which prices are rising | Purchasing power; central banks target ~2% |
| **Unemployment rate** | % of labour force without work but seeking employment | Economic health; social stability |
| **Gini coefficient** | Income inequality measure (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality) | Distribution of prosperity; social cohesion indicator |
| **Trade balance** | Exports minus imports | Positive = surplus; negative = deficit |
| **Government debt-to-GDP** | Total government debt as percentage of GDP | Fiscal sustainability; risk of debt crisis |
| **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)** | Adjusts GDP for cost-of-living differences | Better comparison of actual living standards across countries |
| **Human Development Index (HDI)** | Composite of life expectancy, education, and income | Broader measure of development than GDP alone |

### GDP Limitations

GDP is useful but incomplete:
- **Does not measure**: inequality, environmental damage, unpaid work (caregiving, volunteering), leisure time, quality of life
- **Counts negatives**: disaster recovery and prison spending increase GDP
- **Misses the informal economy**: Black markets, barter, and subsistence farming are undercounted
- **Alternatives being explored**: Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI), Happy Planet Index, Gross National Happiness (Bhutan)

## International Trade

### Why Countries Trade

The principle of **comparative advantage** (David Ricardo, 1817) explains why trade benefits all parties, even when one country is better at producing everything:

| Country | Wine (hours per unit) | Cloth (hours per unit) |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Portugal** | 80 | 90 |
| **England** | 120 | 100 |

Portugal has an absolute advantage in both. But Portugal's *comparative* advantage is wine (80 vs 120 = 33% less effort), while England's comparative advantage is cloth (100 vs 90 = England is relatively better at cloth). Both benefit by specialising and trading.

### Trade Policies

| Policy | What It Does | Effect |
|--------|-------------|--------|
| **Free trade** | No tariffs, quotas, or restrictions | Maximises efficiency; winners and losers within countries |
| **Tariffs** | Tax on imported goods | Protects domestic industries; raises consumer prices; invites retaliation |
| **Quotas** | Limit on quantity of imports | Similar to tariffs but creates scarcity rents |
| **Subsidies** | Government support for domestic producers | Levels the playing field; can distort markets |
| **Embargoes/Sanctions** | Complete ban on trade with a country | Political tool; humanitarian consequences |
| **Trade agreements** | Bilateral or multilateral deals reducing barriers | Increases trade between members; can exclude non-members |

### Major Trade Organisations

| Organisation | Members | Role |
|-------------|---------|------|
| **WTO** (World Trade Organisation) | 164 countries | Sets global trade rules; resolves disputes |
| **IMF** (International Monetary Fund) | 190 countries | Monitors global financial stability; provides emergency loans |
| **World Bank** | 189 countries | Funds development projects in low-income countries |
| **Regional blocs** | EU, USMCA, ASEAN, AfCFTA | Reduce trade barriers within regions |

## Global Supply Chains

### How Modern Supply Chains Work

A single product may involve dozens of countries:

**Example: A smartphone**
| Component | Typical Origin |
|-----------|---------------|
| Chip design | USA (Qualcomm, Apple) |
| Chip fabrication | Taiwan (TSMC), South Korea (Samsung) |
| Memory chips | South Korea (SK Hynix), Japan |
| Display | South Korea (Samsung), China (BOE) |
| Battery | China (ATL), Japan (Murata) |
| Camera sensor | Japan (Sony) |
| Assembly | China (Foxconn), India (growing) |
| Software | USA |
| Brand/marketing | USA, South Korea, China |

### Supply Chain Risks

| Risk | Example | Impact |
|------|---------|--------|
| **Geopolitical** | US-China tensions; Russia-Ukraine war | Chip shortages; energy price spikes |
| **Natural disaster** | 2011 Tōhoku earthquake; Thai floods | Factory shutdowns; cascading delays |
| **Pandemic** | COVID-19 (2020-2022) | Port congestion; semiconductor shortage; inflation |
| **Concentration risk** | TSMC produces ~90% of advanced chips | Single point of failure for global tech |
| **Shipping disruption** | Suez Canal blockage (2021); Red Sea attacks (2024) | $10B/day in delayed trade; rerouting costs |

### Resilience Strategies

| Strategy | Description | Trade-off |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| **Diversification** | Multiple suppliers across regions | Higher cost; more complexity |
| **Nearshoring** | Move production closer to end market | Higher labour costs; shorter supply lines |
| **Friendshoring** | Move production to politically allied countries | Limits supplier options |
| **Inventory buffers** | Hold more safety stock | Higher carrying costs |
| **Vertical integration** | Own more of the supply chain | Less flexibility; more capital required |

## Global Financial System

### How Money Moves Across Borders

| Mechanism | Description | Scale |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **Trade payments** | Importers pay exporters in agreed currency | ~$25 trillion/year in goods |
| **Foreign exchange (Forex)** | Currency trading market | ~$7.5 trillion/day in volume |
| **Remittances** | Workers sending money home | ~$800 billion/year to low- and middle-income countries |
| **Foreign direct investment (FDI)** | Companies investing in other countries | ~$1.3 trillion/year (net inflows to developing countries) |
| **Portfolio investment** | Cross-border stocks, bonds, funds | Trillions; highly volatile |
| **Development aid (ODA)** | Government-to-government assistance | ~$200 billion/year |

### Major Currencies

| Currency | Share of Global Reserves | Key Feature |
|----------|------------------------|-------------|
| **US Dollar (USD)** | ~58% | World reserve currency; most trade invoiced in USD |
| **Euro (EUR)** | ~20% | Second most held; used across 20 EU countries |
| **Japanese Yen (JPY)** | ~5% | Safe-haven currency; carry trade favourite |
| **British Pound (GBP)** | ~5% | Oldest currency still in use; London is a financial hub |
| **Chinese Yuan (CNY)** | ~3% | Growing share; capital controls limit international use |

### Exchange Rates

| Factor | Effect on Currency Value |
|--------|------------------------|
| **Higher interest rates** | Attracts foreign capital → currency appreciates |
| **Strong economic growth** | Attracts investment → currency appreciates |
| **High inflation** | Reduces purchasing power → currency depreciates |
| **Political instability** | Capital flight → currency depreciates |
| **Trade surplus** | More demand for exports → currency appreciates |
| **Government debt** | If unsustainable → currency depreciates |

## Globalisation: Benefits and Tensions

### The Case For Globalisation

| Benefit | Evidence |
|---------|---------|
| **Poverty reduction** | Extreme poverty fell from 36% (1990) to ~8% (2022) — largely driven by trade |
| **Consumer prices** | Access to cheaper goods; increased variety |
| **Innovation diffusion** | Technology and knowledge spread faster across borders |
| **Comparative advantage** | Countries specialise in what they do best; total output increases |
| **Cultural exchange** | Ideas, art, food, and perspectives cross borders |

### The Case Against (or Concerns About) Globalisation

| Concern | Evidence |
|---------|---------|
| **Inequality within countries** | Winners (skilled workers, capital owners) gain more than losers (manufacturing workers in developed countries) |
| **Race to the bottom** | Companies relocate to countries with lowest labour/environmental standards |
| **Cultural homogenisation** | Global brands displace local businesses and traditions |
| **Environmental cost** | Shipping emissions; deforestation for export crops; resource extraction |
| **Vulnerability to shocks** | Interconnected supply chains transmit disruptions globally |
| **Sovereignty concerns** | International organisations may override national democratic decisions |

### Deglobalisation Trends

Recent trends suggest a shift:
- **Reshoring**: Countries bringing critical manufacturing home (semiconductors, pharmaceuticals)
- **Trade wars**: Tariff escalations between major economies
- **Friendshoring**: Trading primarily with political allies
- **Regionalisation**: Strengthening regional trade blocs over global ones
- **Technology decoupling**: Separate tech ecosystems (US vs China in chips, 5G, AI)

## Economic Development: Why Some Countries Are Rich

| Factor | How It Contributes |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Institutions** | Property rights, rule of law, low corruption → investment and innovation flourish |
| **Human capital** | Education and health → productive workforce |
| **Infrastructure** | Roads, ports, electricity, internet → reduces transaction costs |
| **Geography** | Access to navigable waterways, temperate climate, natural resources |
| **Trade access** | Integration into global markets → technology transfer and scale |
| **Stability** | Political stability and sound macroeconomic policy → long-term investment |
| **Innovation ecosystem** | Universities, R&D spending, patent protection → technological frontier |

### Income Classification (World Bank)

| Category | GNI per Capita (2025) | Examples |
|----------|----------------------|---------|
| **Low income** | <$1,145 | Afghanistan, Burundi, Mozambique |
| **Lower-middle income** | $1,146-$4,515 | India, Nigeria, Vietnam |
| **Upper-middle income** | $4,516-$14,005 | China, Brazil, Mexico |
| **High income** | >$14,005 | USA, Germany, Japan, South Korea, Singapore |

## Summary

The global economy is not a zero-sum game — trade creates net benefits, and globalisation has lifted billions out of poverty. But the benefits are unevenly distributed, and the system creates winners and losers within every country. Understanding economic indicators, trade dynamics, supply chain dependencies, and the financial system is essential for making sense of news, policy debates, and business decisions. The key tension of our era is between the efficiency gains of globalisation and the resilience, equity, and sovereignty concerns that are driving a partial retreat from it.
