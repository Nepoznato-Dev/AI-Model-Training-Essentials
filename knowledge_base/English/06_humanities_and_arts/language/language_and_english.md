# Language and English

There are roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world today. English is the most widely spoken as a second language and serves as the default for international business, science, aviation, and the internet. Whether writing code documentation, reading research papers, or communicating across borders, a solid command of English — grammar, vocabulary, and rhetoric — is one of the most practical skills one can develop.

This file covers the essentials: grammar, punctuation, common pitfalls, writing technique, and clear communication.

---

## The Parts of Speech

Every English sentence is built from eight building blocks. Understanding them helps you write clearly and spot errors before they confuse your reader.

| Part of Speech | What It Does | Examples | Common Mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noun | Names a person, place, thing, or idea | city, happiness, algorithm | Confusing abstract vs. concrete nouns |
| Pronoun | Replaces a noun | he, she, it, they, this | Unclear antecedent ("it" — what is "it"?) |
| Verb | Expresses action or state | run, think, is, become | Subject-verb disagreement |
| Adjective | Modifies a noun | fast, complex, beautiful | Using where an adverb is needed |
| Adverb | Modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb | quickly, very, well | "Good" vs. "well" |
| Preposition | Shows relationship between words | in, on, at, by, with | Ending sentences awkwardly (sometimes fine) |
| Conjunction | Connects words, phrases, or clauses | and, but, or, because | Starting sentences with "but" (stylistic, not wrong) |
| Interjection | Expresses emotion | wow, oh, alas | Overuse in formal writing |

## Building Sentences: Clauses and Phrases

A **clause** contains a subject and a verb. A **phrase** does not.

| Type | Can Stand Alone? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Independent clause | Yes | "She wrote the report." |
| Dependent clause | No — needs an independent clause | "Because the deadline was tomorrow..." |
| Prepositional phrase | No | "In the morning" / "On the table" |
| Participial phrase | No | "Running down the street, she tripped." |

**Common error — the sentence fragment:**
- Incorrect: "Because the data was corrupted." (dependent clause standing alone)
- Correct: "The results were invalid because the data was corrupted."

**Common error — the run-on:**
- Incorrect: "She finished the report she submitted it immediately."
- Correct: "She finished the report, then submitted it immediately."

## Punctuation

Punctuation is the traffic signal system of writing. Get it wrong and readers crash.

| Mark | Name | What It Does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| . | Period | Ends a declarative sentence | "The model converged." |
| ? | Question mark | Ends a question | "Did it converge?" |
| ! | Exclamation mark | Ends an exclamatory sentence | "It converged on the first try!" |
| , | Comma | Separates list items, introductory phrases, clauses | "The data was noisy, but the model held." |
| ; | Semicolon | Links two related independent clauses | "The training failed; the learning rate was too high." |
| : | Colon | Introduces a list, explanation, or quotation | "Three problems remained: accuracy, speed, and cost." |
| ' | Apostrophe | Shows possession or contraction | "The model's accuracy" / "don't = do not" |

**Comma rules that trip people up:**
- **Oxford comma** (the comma before "and" in a list): optional but recommended for clarity. "I invited my parents, Beyoncé, and the president" ≠ "I invited my parents, Beyoncé and the president" (the second reads like your parents *are* Beyoncé and the president).
- **Comma after introductory phrases**: "However, the results differed." Not "However the results differed."
- **No comma between a subject and its verb**: "The researcher who designed the experiment was present." Not "The researcher who designed the experiment, was present."

## Subject-Verb Agreement

This is the single most common grammar error in English writing. The rule is simple: **singular subject → singular verb; plural subject → plural verb.** The tricky part is recognising what's actually singular or plural.

### Gerund subjects (verbs acting as nouns) are always singular

| Correct | Incorrect |
|---|---|
| "Eating rocks **is** dangerous." | "Eating rocks **are** dangerous." |
| "Running every day **is** good for health." | "Running every day **are** good for health." |
| "Reading **helps** improve vocabulary." | "Reading **help** improve vocabulary." |

Exception: compound gerund subjects are plural — "Running and swimming **are** good exercises."

### Compound subjects

**Joined by "and"** → plural:
- "Alice and Bob **are** here."

**Joined by "or" / "nor"** → agree with the subject *nearest* the verb:
- "Neither the students nor the teacher **was** ready." (teacher is singular, nearest to verb)
- "Neither the teacher nor the students **were** ready." (students is plural, nearest to verb)

### Collective nouns

Words like *team, committee, group, family* take a **singular** verb in American English:
- "The team **is** practising."
- "The committee **has** made a decision."

British English accepts plural verbs here depending on context — both are fine, just be consistent.

### Indefinite pronouns that are always singular

everyone, everybody, anyone, anybody, someone, somebody, no one, nobody, each, either, neither

- "Everyone **is** invited." (Not "Everyone are invited.")
- "Each of the students **has** completed the assignment."

## Common Confusable Word Pairs

These pairs sound identical but mean different things. Mixing them up is one of the most visible writing errors.

| Pair | Difference | Example |
|---|---|---|
| their / there / they're | possession / location / contraction | "Their files are over there; they're compiling now." |
| your / you're | possession / contraction | "Your code is running — you're almost done." |
| its / it's | possession / contraction | "The model lost its accuracy. It's overfitting." |
| affect / effect | verb (to influence) / noun (the result) | "The bug affected performance. The effect was severe." |
| then / than | time / comparison | "We tested it first, then compared results." |
| who's / whose | contraction / possession | "Who's running the test? Whose dataset is this?" |
| compliment / complement | praise / completing something | "She complimented the design. It complements the brand." |

## Spelling Patterns

English spelling is notoriously irregular, but some rules hold most of the time:

- **"i before e except after c"** — believe, receive, relief — but watch out for exceptions: weird, science, neighbour, caffeine.
- **Silent "e" drop**: Words ending in silent "e" drop it before a vowel suffix — make → making, hope → hopeful. But keep it before a consonant suffix — hope + less = hopeless.
- **Double the final consonant**: When the last syllable is stressed and ends in a single consonant — run → running, prefer → preferred. But not if the stress isn't on the last syllable — visit → visiting (not "visitted").

## Vocabulary and Word Formation

English is a linguistic sponge — it has borrowed heavily from Latin, Greek, French, and Germanic roots over centuries. Understanding roots, prefixes, and suffixes lets you decode unfamiliar words.

**Common roots:**

| Root | Origin | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| dict- | Latin | to say | dictate, predict, contradict |
| graph- / -graph | Greek | to write | graphic, biography, photograph |
| struct- | Latin | to build | structure, construct, infrastructure |
| -logy | Greek | study of | biology, psychology, technology |
| -tion / -sion | Latin | the act of | creation, discussion, revision |

**Prefixes that change meaning:**

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| un- | not | uncertain, unhappy, undo |
| re- | again | rewrite, reconsider, rebuild |
| pre- | before | preview, precompute, pretrain |
| mis- | wrongly | misunderstand, misclassify, mislead |
| over- | too much | overfit, overestimate, overengineer |

**Figurative language:**

| Device | How It Works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Metaphor | Says something *is* something else | "Time is a thief." |
| Simile | Compares using "like" or "as" | "Busy as a bee." |
| Hyperbole | Deliberate exaggeration | "I've told you a million times." |
| Personification | Giving human traits to non-human things | "The data spoke for itself." |

## Writing Clearly

Effective writing is the result of clear thinking made visible.

**The paragraph structure:**
1. **Topic sentence** — tells the reader what this paragraph is about
2. **Supporting sentences** — evidence, examples, explanation
3. **Concluding sentence** — wraps up or transitions to the next idea

**The revision process:**

| Stage | Focus | What You're Looking For |
|---|---|---|
| Drafting | Getting ideas down | Don't edit yet — just write |
| Revision | Structure and argument | Does the logic flow? Are ideas in the right order? |
| Editing | Grammar and clarity | Awkward sentences, word choice, consistency |
| Proofreading | Surface errors | Typos, missing commas, formatting |

**Principles that improve most writing immediately:**
- Cut filler words: "in order to" → "to"; "due to the fact that" → "because"
- Use active voice: "The model achieved 95% accuracy" beats "95% accuracy was achieved by the model"
- One idea per sentence. One topic per paragraph.
- Read it aloud — if you stumble, your reader will too.

## Rhetoric: The Art of Persuasion

Whether you're writing a research paper, a product proposal, or an email asking for a raise, you're doing rhetoric — the art of effective communication.

**Aristotle's three appeals:**

| Appeal | Targets | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ethos | Credibility | "Based on 10 years of clinical trials..." |
| Pathos | Emotion | "Imagine a world where no child goes hungry." |
| Logos | Logic | "Revenue grew 40% year-over-year, with margins expanding by 5 points." |

The most persuasive communication uses all three. Pure logic without emotion feels cold. Pure emotion without evidence feels hollow. Credibility (ethos) is what makes people trust you enough to listen.

**Register** — the level of formality — matters too. "Hey, so the model's kinda broken lol" and "The model exhibits significant performance degradation" convey the same information but to very different audiences. Match your register to your context.

## Epistemic Honesty: Knowing When to Say "I Don't Know"

One of the most important intellectual habits: being honest about the limits of your knowledge. Saying "I don't have reliable information on that" is always better than producing a confident-sounding but false answer.

**Hallucination** — generating plausible-sounding but fabricated information — is a well-known failure mode of AI systems, but humans do it too. The fix is the same: recognise when you're outside your expertise and say so.

**Useful phrases for honest uncertainty:**
- "I'm not certain, but…"
- "You may want to verify this with a specialist."
- "I don't have enough information to answer reliably."
- "This is outside my area of expertise."

**For health, legal, and safety questions:** Always defer to qualified professionals. Stating a recommended dose, a legal ruling, or a medical treatment without professional context can cause real harm.

## Reading and Comprehension

Writing and reading are two sides of the same skill. Active reading — engaging with the text rather than passively scanning — dramatically improves comprehension and retention.

| Strategy | What It Looks Like |
|---|---|
| Questioning | "What is the author trying to prove? What evidence do they give?" |
| Summarising | After each section, restate the main idea in your own words |
| Connecting | "This reminds me of..." / "This contradicts what I read about..." |
| Predicting | "Based on this argument, the conclusion will probably be..." |
| Context clues | Using surrounding words to figure out an unfamiliar term |

The **main idea** is the central point. Everything else — examples, data, anecdotes — supports it. Learning to separate the main idea from the supporting details is the foundation of critical reading.
