# Philosophy and Critical Thinking

Philosophy is the systematic study of fundamental questions about knowledge, reality, and existence. It provides the framework for evaluating what counts as knowledge, what is worth doing, and how to distinguish well-reasoned arguments from weak ones. Critical thinking is the practical application of this framework: the skill of analysing claims, identifying flawed reasoning, and making better-informed decisions. This file covers the major ideas, the tools for evaluating them, and the cognitive biases that affect everyone.

---

## Major Schools of Thought

Philosophy has thousands of years of tradition. Most of the important positions can be grouped into a handful of schools.

### Ancient and Classical

| School | Core Idea | Key Figure |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Stoicism** | Focus on what you can control; accept what you can't. Virtue is the only good. | Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Seneca |
| **Epicureanism** | The good life is simple pleasures, friendship, and freedom from anxiety. | Epicurus |
| **Cynicism** | Reject conventional desires and social norms; live in accordance with nature. | Diogenes |
| **Skepticism** | Suspend judgment — certainty is impossible or at least very hard. | Pyrrho, Sextus Empiricus |
| **Confucianism** | Social harmony through moral cultivation, ritual, and proper relationships. | Confucius |
| **Taoism** | Live in harmony with the Tao (the Way); effortless action (wu wei). | Laozi |
| **Buddhism** | Suffering comes from attachment; liberation through the Eightfold Path. | Siddhartha Gautama |

### Modern Philosophy

| School | Core Idea | Key Figure |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Rationalism** | Knowledge comes primarily from reason and innate ideas. | Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz |
| **Empiricism** | Knowledge comes from sensory experience. The mind is a blank slate. | Locke, Hume, Berkeley |
| **Idealism** | Reality is fundamentally mental; the material world is secondary. | Kant (transcendental), Hegel (absolute) |
| **Pragmatism** | The meaning and truth of an idea lie in its practical consequences. | Peirce, James, Dewey |
| **Existentialism** | Existence precedes essence. We create our own meaning through choices. | Sartre, de Beauvoir, Camus, Kierkegaard |
| **Phenomenology** | Study of conscious experience from the first-person perspective. | Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty |
| **Analytic Philosophy** | Clarify language and logic; dominant in the English-speaking world. | Russell, Wittgenstein, Frege |

### Contemporary

| School | Core Idea | Key Figure |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Utilitarianism** | The right action maximises overall well-being. | Bentham, Mill, Singer |
| **Deontology** | Some actions are inherently right or wrong, regardless of consequences. | Kant |
| **Virtue Ethics** | Focus on character rather than rules or outcomes. | Aristotle, MacIntyre |
| **Postmodernism** | Grand narratives are suspect; truth is shaped by power and context. | Foucault, Derrida, Lyotard |
| **Nihilism** | Life has no inherent meaning, value, or purpose. | Nietzsche (diagnosed, didn't endorse) |
| **Absurdism** | The universe is meaningless, but we should live defiantly anyway. | Camus |

## Ethics Frameworks

When you need to decide what's right, you're doing ethics — whether you realise it or not. There are three main approaches.

### The Three Frameworks

| Framework | Question It Asks | Strength | Weakness |
|-----------|-----------------|----------|----------|
| **Consequentialism** (Utilitarianism) | "What produces the best outcome?" | Practical, measurable, scales to policy | Hard to predict consequences; can justify harming minorities |
| **Deontology** (Kantian ethics) | "What rule should everyone follow?" | Protects individual rights; consistent | Rigid; conflicting duties |
| **Virtue Ethics** | "What would a good person do?" | Flexible; focuses on character | Vague; doesn't give clear action guidance |

### Kant's Categorical Imperative

Kant's test for whether an action is moral:

1. **Universalisability**: Could you will that everyone act this way? (If lying were universal, trust would collapse.)
2. **Humanity as an end**: Never treat people merely as means to an end.

### Applied Ethics

| Domain | Key Questions |
|--------|--------------|
| **Bioethics** | Abortion, euthanasia, genetic engineering, animal rights |
| **Business ethics** | Corporate responsibility, whistleblowing, fair wages |
| **Tech/AI ethics** | Privacy, algorithmic bias, autonomous weapons, job displacement |
| **Environmental ethics** | Climate responsibility, animal welfare, intergenerational justice |

## Logic and Argumentation

Logic is the study of valid reasoning. It gives you the tools to tell when someone's argument actually supports their conclusion — and when it doesn't.

### Types of Reasoning

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Deductive** | If premises are true, conclusion *must* be true. | All humans are mortal. Socrates is human. → Socrates is mortal. |
| **Inductive** | Premises make conclusion *probable*, not certain. | Every swan I've seen is white. → Probably all swans are white. |
| **Abductive** | Inference to the best explanation. | The grass is wet. Best explanation: it rained. |

### Common Logical Fallacies

| Fallacy | Description | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| **Ad hominem** | Attacking the person, not the argument | "You can't trust her on economics — she's not an economist." |
| **Straw man** | Misrepresenting an argument to make it easier to attack | "You want to reduce the military budget? So you want to leave the country defenceless?" |
| **Appeal to authority** | "X is true because an expert said so" (without checking the argument) | "This Nobel laureate says this about politics, so it must be right." |
| **False dichotomy** | Presenting only two options when more exist | "Either you support this policy or you don't care about children." |
| **Slippery slope** | A leads to Z through a chain of unlikely events | "If we legalise marijuana, soon we'll legalise everything." |
| **Begging the question** | The conclusion is assumed in the premises | "This law is unjust because it's unfair." (Unjust = unfair — same thing) |
| **Post hoc ergo propter hoc** | A happened before B, so A caused B | "I took a supplement, then I felt better — the supplement worked." |
| **Tu quoque** | "You do it too" — dismissing criticism because the critic is hypocritical | "You tell me to eat healthier, but you eat fast food." |
| **Appeal to nature** | "Natural = good" | "Humans evolved to eat meat, so veganism is unnatural and wrong." |
| **Sunk cost fallacy** | Continuing because of past investment, not future value | "We've spent $10M on this project; we can't stop now." |

### Evaluating Arguments

A good argument has:
1. **True (or well-supported) premises**
2. **Valid logic** (the conclusion follows from the premises)
3. **No hidden assumptions**
4. **Relevance** (the premises actually address the conclusion)

## Epistemology: What Can We Know?

Epistemology is the study of knowledge itself — what it is, how we get it, and what its limits are.

### Sources of Knowledge

| Source | Description | Limitation |
|--------|-------------|------------|
| **Perception** | Direct sensory experience | Illusions, hallucinations, limited range |
| **Reason** | Logical deduction and mathematical proof | Only tells you what follows from your premises |
| **Introspection** | Examining your own thoughts and feelings | Self-deception; limited access to your own processes |
| **Testimony** | What other people tell you | People lie, are mistaken, or oversimplify |
| **Memory** | Recalling past experiences | Unreliable; reconstructive; degrades over time |
| **Scientific method** | Systematic observation, hypothesis testing, replication | Slow; limited to testable questions; paradigm-dependent |

### Key Epistemological Problems

| Problem | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **The problem of induction** | Just because the sun rose every day in the past doesn't *logically* guarantee it will tomorrow (Hume) |
| **Gettier problems** | Justified true belief may not be knowledge — you can be right by luck |
| **The demarcation problem** | What distinguishes science from pseudoscience? (Popper: falsifiability) |
| **Underdetermination** | Evidence alone may not uniquely determine which theory is correct |

## Philosophy of Mind

| Position | Claim | Challenge |
|----------|-------|-----------|
| **Dualism** | Mind and body are separate substances | How do they interact? (The interaction problem) |
| **Physicalism/Materialism** | Everything is physical; consciousness is a brain process | The hard problem: why does experience *feel* like anything? |
| **Functionalism** | Mental states are defined by their functional role, not their substrate | Could a sufficiently complex computer be conscious? |
| **Panpsychism** | Consciousness is a fundamental feature of all matter | How does micro-consciousness combine into macro-consciousness? |
| **Illusionism** | Consciousness as we experience it is an illusion | Seems to deny the one thing we're most certain of |

### Key Thought Experiments

| Experiment | Point |
|-----------|-------|
| **Turing Test** | If a machine's responses are indistinguishable from a human's, is it thinking? |
| **Chinese Room** (Searle) | Manipulating symbols ≠ understanding. Syntax is not semantics. |
| **Mary's Room** (Jackson) | A scientist who knows all physical facts about colour still learns something new when she sees red for the first time. |
| **Philosophical Zombie** | A being physically identical to you but with no inner experience — is this conceivable? |
| **Trolley Problem** | Is it morally acceptable to sacrifice one person to save five? Tests intuitions about consequentialism vs deontology. |

## Cognitive Biases

Your brain takes shortcuts. Most of the time, they work well. When they don't, the results are called cognitive biases. Knowing them is the first step to avoiding them.

### Decision-Making Biases

| Bias | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Confirmation bias** | Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs | Only reading news sources that agree with you |
| **Anchoring** | Over-relying on the first piece of information encountered | Negotiating from an initial price that's too high |
| **Availability heuristic** | Judging probability by how easily examples come to mind | Overestimating plane crash risk because they're widely reported |
| **Dunning-Kruger effect** | Low-ability people overestimate their ability; experts underestimate theirs | A beginner confident they can solve a problem an expert finds hard |
| **Framing effect** | Decisions change based on how options are presented | "90% survival rate" sounds better than "10% mortality rate" |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses feel roughly twice as bad as equivalent gains feel good | Holding a losing stock because selling would "lock in" the loss |
| **Status quo bias** | Preferring things to stay the same | Not switching to a better plan because changing is effort |

### Social Biases

| Bias | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Halo effect** | One positive trait colours your overall impression of a person |
| **Fundamental attribution error** | Attributing others' behaviour to character, your own to circumstances |
| **In-group bias** | Favouring people you perceive as part of your group |
| **Bandwagon effect** | Adopting beliefs because many others hold them |
| **Just-world fallacy** | Believing people get what they deserve |

### How to Mitigate Biases

1. **Steel-man the opposition**: Construct the strongest version of the argument you disagree with.
2. **Seek disconfirming evidence**: Actively look for reasons you might be wrong.
3. **Consider base rates**: How common is this type of outcome in general?
4. **Use checklists and frameworks**: Externalise your thinking to catch blind spots.
5. **Sleep on important decisions**: First instincts are often biased ones.
6. **Invite disagreement**: Surround yourself with people who will tell you when you're wrong.

## Why This Matters

Philosophy and critical thinking aren't abstract exercises. They're practical tools:

- **Ethics frameworks** help you navigate real dilemmas — in business, technology, and personal life.
- **Logic** helps you spot bad arguments in politics, advertising, and social media.
- **Cognitive biases** explain why capable people make systematic errors in judgment — and how to reduce their frequency.
- **Epistemology** is increasingly relevant in an era of misinformation: how can the truth of a claim be evaluated?

The study of philosophy does not require agreement with any particular philosopher. It requires the ability to examine beliefs and the reasoning behind them.
