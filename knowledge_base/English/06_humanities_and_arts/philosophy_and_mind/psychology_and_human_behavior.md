# Psychology and Human Behavior

Psychology is the science of how and why people think, feel, and act the way they do. It is not mind-reading, and it is not common sense — though it sometimes confirms what common sense suggests and sometimes contradicts it entirely. Psychology uses the same scientific method as every other science: observe, hypothesize, test, revise. What makes it challenging is that its subject — the human mind — is the most complex object we know of.

This file covers the major areas of psychology: how we think, how we develop, how we are influenced by others, what goes wrong, and what can be done about it.

---

## How Psychology Works — Major Approaches

Psychology is not one unified theory. It is a collection of perspectives, each of which explains different aspects of behavior:

| Approach | Focus | Key Idea |
|----------|-------|----------|
| **Biological** | Brain structures, neurotransmitters, genetics | Behavior is caused by physical processes in the brain |
| **Cognitive** | Thinking, memory, perception, decision-making | Behavior is shaped by how we process information |
| **Behavioral** | Observable behavior and learning | Behavior is learned through rewards, punishments, and associations |
| **Psychodynamic** | Unconscious drives, early experiences | Behavior is influenced by unconscious conflicts (originated with Freud) |
| **Humanistic** | Personal growth, self-actualization | People have an innate drive toward growth and fulfillment |
| **Social-cultural** | How environment and culture shape behavior | Behavior cannot be understood outside its social context |

Most modern psychologists are **eclectic** — they draw from multiple approaches depending on the question.

## Cognitive Psychology — How You Think

Your brain is not a camera. It does not record reality accurately and play it back. It constructs your experience of the world from incomplete sensory data, past experiences, expectations, and emotions. This construction is usually good enough — but it is also systematically flawed in predictable ways.

### Memory

Memory is not a filing cabinet. It is more like a reconstruction — every time you remember something, you are rebuilding it, not retrieving a perfect copy.

| Stage | What Happens | Limitation |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| **Encoding** | Information enters the system | You only encode what you pay attention to |
| **Storage** | Information is retained | Memories change over time; details are lost or added |
| **Retrieval** | Information is accessed | Retrieval cues matter; context-dependent memory |

**Short-term memory** holds about 7 items (±2) for about 20 seconds. **Working memory** is the active system that manipulates information — it is what you are using right now to follow this sentence. **Long-term memory** has essentially unlimited capacity but requires repetition, emotional significance, or deep processing to encode.

Key findings:
- **Spacing effect**: Distributed practice (studying over time) is far more effective than cramming.
- **Testing effect**: Retrieving information from memory strengthens it more than re-reading.
- **Misinformation effect**: Leading questions can alter memories. Elizabeth Loftus demonstrated that witnesses can "remember" events that never happened.
- **Flashbulb memories**: Vivid emotional memories feel permanent but are still subject to distortion.

### Cognitive Biases

Your brain takes shortcuts. Most of the time, these shortcuts work. When they do not, the result is a cognitive bias. These are not stupid mistakes — they are built into the architecture of human cognition.

| Bias | What Happens | Real-World Impact |
|------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Confirmation bias** | Seek information that confirms existing beliefs | Political polarization; poor scientific reasoning |
| **Anchoring** | Over-rely on the first piece of information | Salary negotiations; price judgments |
| **Availability heuristic** | Judge probability by how easily examples come to mind | Fear of flying (dramatic) vs. driving (statistically dangerous) |
| **Dunning-Kruger effect** | Low-ability people overestimate their skill; experts underestimate theirs | Overconfidence in unfamiliar domains |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses feel about twice as bad as equivalent gains feel good | Holding losing investments; resisting change |
| **Sunk cost fallacy** | Continue investing because of past investment, not future value | Staying in bad relationships; throwing good money after bad |
| **Framing effect** | Decisions change based on how options are presented | "90% survival rate" vs. "10% mortality rate" |
| **Hindsight bias** | "I knew it all along" — after the fact | Overconfidence in prediction ability |

## Developmental Psychology — How People Grow

Humans are born highly undeveloped compared to other mammals. A foal walks within hours of birth; a human infant takes over a year. This extended development period allows considerable flexibility and learning — but it also means that early experiences carry significant weight.

### Key Developmental Stages

| Stage | Age | What Develops | Key Theorist |
|-------|-----|---------------|-------------|
| **Sensorimotor** | 0-2 years | Object permanence (things exist when not seen) | Piaget |
| **Preoperational** | 2-7 years | Language, symbolic thinking; egocentric (cannot see others' perspectives) | Piaget |
| **Concrete operational** | 7-11 years | Logical thinking about concrete events; conservation | Piaget |
| **Formal operational** | 11+ years | Abstract thinking, hypothetical reasoning | Piaget |
| **Trust vs. mistrust** | 0-1 year | Does the world provide? | Erikson |
| **Identity vs. role confusion** | 12-18 years | Who am I? | Erikson |

### Nature and Nurture

The nature-nurture question — "is it genetics or environment?" — has been resolved: it is both, in all cases, and they interact. Genes set a range of possibilities; environment determines where within that range a person falls. Identical twins raised apart show considerable similarity (suggesting strong genetic influence on personality, intelligence, and even preferences), but they are not identical (indicating that environment also plays a role).

## Social Psychology — How Other People Change You

You are not the same person in a group as you are alone. Social psychology studies how the presence of others — real or imagined — changes how you think, feel, and act.

### The Classic Experiments

These experiments revealed uncomfortable truths about human behavior:

| Experiment | What It Showed | Why It Matters |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Asch conformity** (1951) | 75% of people conformed to an incorrect group answer at least once | Social pressure can override individual perception |
| **Milgram obedience** (1961) | 65% of people administered what they believed were lethal electric shocks when instructed by an authority figure | Ordinary people can cause significant harm when following orders |
| **Stanford Prison** (1971) | "Guards" became abusive and "prisoners" became passive within days | Roles and situations can override personality (methodology is now heavily criticized) |
| **Bystander effect** (Darley & Latané, 1968) | People are less likely to help when others are present | Diffusion of responsibility; the more witnesses, the less likely anyone acts |

### Social Influence

| Phenomenon | What It Is | Example |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| **Conformity** | Adjusting behavior to match the group | Dressing like your peers; agreeing with the majority |
| **Obedience** | Complying with authority figures | Following orders from a boss, even when questionable |
| **Groupthink** | Groups prioritize harmony over critical evaluation | Bad decisions because nobody wanted to disagree |
| **Social loafing** | Putting in less effort when working in a group | Group projects where one person does most of the work |
| **Fundamental attribution error** | Attributing others' behavior to personality while attributing your own to circumstances | "They are lazy" vs. "I was tired" |

## Personality — What Makes You, You

Personality is the consistent pattern of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that makes you recognizable as the same person across situations and over time.

### The Big Five

The most scientifically supported model of personality identifies five dimensions. Everyone falls somewhere on a spectrum for each:

| Trait | High End | Low End | What It Predicts |
|-------|----------|---------|-----------------|
| **Openness** | Curious, creative, open to new experiences | Practical, prefers routine | Artistic interests; political views |
| **Conscientiousness** | Organized, dependable, disciplined | Flexible, spontaneous | Academic performance; health behaviors; job performance |
| **Extraversion** | Sociable, energetic, seeks stimulation | Reserved, reflective | Social relationships; career choices |
| **Agreeableness** | Cooperative, trusting, compassionate | Competitive, skeptical | Relationship quality; conflict resolution |
| **Neuroticism** | Emotionally reactive, anxious, mood swings | Emotionally stable, resilient | Mental health; relationship satisfaction |

Personality is roughly 40-60% heritable and relatively stable after age 30 — but it is not fixed. People generally become more conscientious and agreeable and less neurotic as they age.

## Mental Health — When the System Breaks Down

Mental health disorders are not character flaws or weaknesses. They are medical conditions — often with biological foundations — that affect how people think, feel, and function. They are extremely common: roughly 1 in 5 adults experience a mental health condition in any given year.

### Major Categories

| Category | Examples | Core Feature | Treatment |
|----------|---------|-------------|-----------|
| **Anxiety disorders** | Generalized anxiety, panic disorder, phobias, social anxiety | Excessive fear or worry that interferes with functioning | CBT, medication (SSRIs), exposure therapy |
| **Mood disorders** | Depression, bipolar disorder | Persistent emotional states (sadness or mood swings) | Medication, psychotherapy, lifestyle changes |
| **Psychotic disorders** | Schizophrenia | Distorted thinking, perception, and reality | Antipsychotic medication, therapy, social support |
| **Trauma-related** | PTSD | Re-experiencing, avoidance, hyperarousal after trauma | Trauma-focused CBT, EMDR, medication |
| **Eating disorders** | Anorexia, bulimia, binge-eating | Disturbed eating behaviors and body image | Therapy, nutritional counseling, medication |
| **Personality disorders** | Borderline, antisocial, narcissistic | Inflexible patterns causing significant problems | Specialized therapy (DBT for BPD), long-term treatment |

### What Works in Treatment

| Approach | How It Works | Best For |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| **CBT (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy)** | Identify and change distorted thought patterns | Anxiety, depression, phobias — most evidence-based |
| **Psychodynamic therapy** | Explore unconscious patterns and early experiences | Relationship patterns, recurring problems |
| **Medication** | Alter brain chemistry (SSRIs, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics) | Moderate-to-severe disorders; often combined with therapy |
| **Lifestyle changes** | Exercise, sleep, nutrition, social connection | All conditions; foundational for mental health |
| **Mindfulness/meditation** | Train attention and reduce reactivity | Anxiety, stress, depression relapse prevention |

The most effective treatment for most conditions is a combination of therapy and (when appropriate) medication, along with lifestyle factors — exercise, sleep, and social connection.

## Motivation and Emotion

### What Drives Behavior

- **Intrinsic motivation**: Doing something because it is inherently satisfying (curiosity, mastery, purpose). Generally produces better long-term outcomes than extrinsic motivation.
- **Extrinsic motivation**: Doing something for external rewards (money, grades, praise). Effective for simple tasks but can actually undermine intrinsic motivation for complex or creative tasks (the "overjustification effect").
- **Maslow's hierarchy**: Physiological needs → safety → belonging → esteem → self-actualization. The idea that you must satisfy lower needs before higher ones is intuitive but not strongly supported by research — people pursue meaning even in extreme deprivation.

### Emotions

Emotions are not irrational noise — they are evolved responses that serve specific functions. Fear makes you flee from danger. Disgust makes you avoid contamination. Anger mobilizes you to defend against threats. Sadness signals a need for support.

The **fight-or-flight response** (activation of the sympathetic nervous system) is the body's reaction to perceived threats. It is adaptive in short bursts but harmful when chronically activated — chronic stress increases risk of heart disease, depression, immune dysfunction, and cognitive decline.

## Learning — How Behavior Changes

Learning is not just what happens in classrooms. It is how all behavior is shaped by experience.

### Three Types of Learning

| Type | Mechanism | Key Researcher | Example |
|------|-----------|---------------|---------|
| **Classical conditioning** | Association between stimuli | Pavlov (dogs salivating at a bell) | Feeling anxious when you hear a specific sound associated with a past trauma |
| **Operant conditioning** | Behavior shaped by consequences | Skinner (rats pressing levers for food) | Working harder for bonuses; children cleaning rooms for allowance |
| **Observational learning** | Learning by watching others | Bandura (Bobo doll experiment) | Children imitating parents; learning skills from YouTube |

**Reinforcement** (anything that increases a behavior) is more effective than **punishment** (anything that decreases a behavior). The most effective reinforcement schedules are variable (unpredictable) — this is why slot machines are addictive and why social media notifications are so compelling.

## Summary

Psychology reveals that the human mind is both extraordinarily capable and systematically flawed. We can learn language, solve complex problems, create art, and build civilizations — but we are also vulnerable to biases, social pressure, and emotional patterns that sometimes work against us. Understanding psychology is not about becoming a therapist. It is about understanding why you make the decisions you make, why you feel what you feel, and how the social and physical environment shapes your behavior. That understanding is useful in every area of life — relationships, work, parenting, and making sense of a world that often seems irrational.
