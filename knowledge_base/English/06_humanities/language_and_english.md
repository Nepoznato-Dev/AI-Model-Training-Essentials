# Language and English

## What is Language?

Language is a system of communication using sounds, symbols, or gestures that follows a shared set of rules understood by a community. Human language is unique in its complexity, creativity, and capacity to express abstract ideas. There are approximately 7,000 languages spoken in the world today. English is the most widely spoken language as a second language and is the dominant language of international communication, business, science, and the internet.

## English Grammar

Grammar is the set of rules governing how words are combined to form sentences in a language. A sentence must contain a subject (who or what the sentence is about) and a predicate (what the subject does or is).

A **noun** is a word that names a person, place, thing, or idea.
A **verb** is a word that expresses an action or state of being.
An **adjective** modifies a noun.
An **adverb** modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb.
A **pronoun** takes the place of a noun.

A **clause** is a group of words containing a subject and a verb. An independent clause can stand alone as a sentence. A dependent clause cannot stand alone and depends on an independent clause. A **phrase** is a group of words that does not contain both a subject and a predicate.

## Parts of Speech

The eight traditional parts of speech in English are: noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, and interjection.

A **preposition** shows the relationship between a noun or pronoun and another word (e.g., in, on, at, by, with).
A **conjunction** connects words, phrases, or clauses (e.g., and, but, or, because).

**Common coordinating conjunctions** can be remembered with the acronym FANBOYS:
- **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o

## Punctuation

- A period (.) ends a declarative sentence.
- A question mark (?) ends an interrogative sentence.
- An exclamation mark (!) ends an exclamatory sentence.
- A comma (,) separates items in a list, sets off introductory phrases, and separates independent clauses joined by a conjunction.
- A semicolon (;) separates two closely related independent clauses.
- A colon (:) introduces a list, explanation, or quotation.
- An apostrophe (') indicates possession (John's book) or a contraction (don't = do not).

## Spelling and Common Errors

**Common spelling rules:**
- "i before e except after c" (believe, receive) — though this rule has many exceptions (e.g., "weird", "science")
- Words ending in a silent "e" drop the "e" before adding a vowel suffix (make → making)
- Double the final consonant before adding a vowel suffix when the last syllable is stressed and ends in a single consonant (run → running)

**Common confusable word pairs:**
- their / there / they're
- your / you're
- its / it's
- affect / effect
- then / than

## Vocabulary and Word Formation

**Etymology** is the study of the origins and historical development of words. Many English words come from Latin, Greek, French, and Germanic roots.

**Prefixes** are added to the beginning of words to change their meaning (e.g., un-, re-, pre-, mis-).
**Suffixes** are added to the end of words (e.g., -tion, -ness, -ing, -ly).

- **Synonyms**: words with similar meanings
- **Antonyms**: words with opposite meanings
- **Metaphor**: describes something by saying it is something else ("Time is a thief")
- **Simile**: compares two things using "like" or "as" ("Busy as a bee")

## Writing Skills

Good writing is clear, concise, coherent, and engaging.

- A **paragraph** should have a topic sentence (the main idea), supporting sentences, and a concluding sentence.
- The **five-paragraph essay** structure consists of an introduction, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion.
- **Revision** is the process of improving a draft.
- **Editing** focuses on correcting grammar, spelling, and punctuation errors.
- **Proofreading** is the final check before publication.

## Reading and Comprehension

**Active reading** involves engaging with the text by asking questions, making predictions, summarizing, and making connections.

- The **main idea** is the central point of a passage.
- Details, examples, and evidence support the main idea.
- **Inference** involves drawing conclusions from information that is implied but not stated directly.
- **Context clues** are words and phrases surrounding an unfamiliar word that help determine its meaning.

## Communication and Rhetoric

**Rhetoric** is the art of effective communication and persuasion.
- **Ethos** appeals to the credibility of the speaker.
- **Pathos** appeals to emotion.
- **Logos** appeals to logic and reason.

A **thesis statement** clearly expresses the main argument of an essay.
**Tone** refers to the writer's attitude toward the subject and audience.
**Register** refers to the level of formality in language use, ranging from casual conversation to formal academic writing.

## Subject-Verb Agreement (Expanded)

One of the most important grammar rules in English is that a subject and its verb must agree in number: a singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb.

### Gerunds as Subjects

A gerund (a verb used as a noun, ending in -ing) is always singular.

**Correct:**
- "Eating rocks **is** dangerous."
- "Running every day **is** good for your health."
- "Reading **helps** improve vocabulary."

**Incorrect:**
- "Eating rocks **are** dangerous." (gerund is singular)
- "Running and swimming **is** good." (compound subject — plural, use "are")

### Compound Subjects

- Joined by "and": plural verb
  - "Alice and Bob **are** here."
  - "The teacher and the student **agree**."

- Joined by "or"/"nor": agree with the subject closest to the verb
  - "Neither the students nor the teacher **was** ready."
  - "Neither the teacher nor the students **were** ready."

### Collective Nouns

Collective nouns (team, committee, group, family) take a singular verb in American English:
- "The team **is** practising."
- "The committee **has** made a decision."

In British English, plural verbs are also accepted depending on context.

### Indefinite Pronouns

The following indefinite pronouns are **always singular**:
- everyone, everybody, anyone, anybody, someone, somebody, no one, nobody
- each, either, neither

**Correct:**
- "Everyone **is** invited."
- "Each of the students **has** completed the assignment."

**Incorrect:**
- "Everyone **are** invited."

## Epistemic Honesty — Knowing When to Say "I Don't Know"

**Epistemic honesty** means being truthful about the limits of one's knowledge. It is better to say "I don't have reliable information on that" than to produce a confident-sounding but false answer.

**Hallucination** is the tendency of AI systems to generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect statements. Guarding against hallucination requires recognising when a question falls outside available knowledge and responding with an honest acknowledgement of uncertainty rather than an invented answer.

**Useful phrases for expressing uncertainty:**
- "I'm not certain, but…"
- "You may want to verify this with a specialist."
- "I don't have enough information to answer reliably."
- "This is outside my knowledge base."

**For health, medical, legal, or safety questions:**
It is always better to defer to qualified professionals rather than offer specific advice. Stating a recommended dose of a substance, a treatment for a disease, or a legal ruling without professional context can cause real harm and must be avoided.
