# Psychology and Human Behavior

## Introduction to Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behavior. It examines how people think, feel, act, and interact. Major perspectives in psychology include biological (examining brain structures and neurotransmitters), behavioral (focusing on observable behaviors and learning), cognitive (studying mental processes like memory and decision-making), psychodynamic (exploring unconscious drives and conflicts, originating with Freud), humanistic (emphasizing personal growth and self-actualization), and social-cultural (examining how environment and culture shape behavior). Psychology uses scientific methods including experiments, observations, surveys, and case studies to understand human experience.

## Cognitive Psychology

Cognitive psychology studies mental processes including attention, perception, memory, language, problem-solving, and decision-making. Attention is the ability to focus on specific stimuli while filtering out others. Perception involves organizing and interpreting sensory information. Memory has three main stages: encoding (getting information in), storage (retaining information), and retrieval (getting information out). Short-term memory holds limited information briefly; long-term memory has vast capacity for extended periods. Working memory temporarily manipulates information for complex tasks. Language acquisition involves understanding and producing communication. Problem-solving strategies include algorithms (step-by-step procedures) and heuristics (mental shortcuts). Decision-making can be influenced by biases and emotions.

## Developmental Psychology

Developmental psychology examines how people change and grow throughout their lifespan. Prenatal development includes germinal, embryonic, and fetal stages. Infancy (0-2 years) features rapid physical growth, attachment formation, and sensorimotor learning. Childhood (2-12 years) involves language development, social skills, and concrete thinking. Adolescence (12-18 years) brings puberty, identity formation, and abstract reasoning. Early adulthood (20s-30s) focuses on career, relationships, and independence. Middle adulthood (40s-60s) often involves career consolidation and caring for aging parents. Late adulthood (65+) may include retirement, health challenges, and reflection. Key theories include Piaget's stages of cognitive development, Erikson's psychosocial stages, and Kohlberg's moral development theory. Nature (genetics) and nurture (environment) both influence development.

## Social Psychology

Social psychology studies how individuals think, feel, and behave in social contexts. Conformity is adjusting behavior to match group norms, demonstrated in Asch's line experiments. Obedience is complying with authority figures, studied in Milgram's shock experiments. Social facilitation improves performance on simple tasks when others watch. Social loafing reduces effort in groups. Groupthink occurs when groups prioritize harmony over critical evaluation. Prejudice is preconceived judgment about groups; discrimination is unequal treatment. Stereotypes are generalized beliefs about groups. Attribution theory explains how we interpret others' behavior (internal vs. external causes). The fundamental attribution error overestimates personality and underestimates situation when judging others. Bystander effect reduces helping behavior when others are present. Aggression can be influenced by biology, frustration, and social learning. Attraction involves proximity, similarity, physical attractiveness, and reciprocity.

## Personality Psychology

Personality refers to enduring patterns of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. The Big Five personality traits are openness (curiosity, creativity), conscientiousness (organization, dependability), extraversion (sociability, energy from others), agreeableness (cooperation, compassion), and neuroticism (emotional instability, anxiety). Freud's psychoanalytic theory proposed id (instincts), ego (reality), and superego (morality). Humanistic theories (Maslow, Rogers) emphasize growth potential and self-actualization. Trait theories identify consistent characteristics across situations. Type theories categorize people (e.g., Type A vs. Type B behavior patterns). Personality develops through genetics, environment, and experiences. Assessment tools include self-report inventories (MMPI) and projective tests (Rorschach inkblots).

## Mental Health and Disorders

Mental health encompasses emotional, psychological, and social well-being. Psychological disorders are patterns causing distress or impaired functioning. Anxiety disorders include generalized anxiety, panic disorder, phobias, and social anxiety. Mood disorders include depression (persistent sadness) and bipolar disorder (mood swings). Schizophrenia involves distorted thinking, perceptions, and emotions. Eating disorders include anorexia nervosa, bulimia, and binge-eating. Personality disorders involve inflexible patterns causing problems (e.g., borderline, antisocial). Trauma-related disorders include PTSD following traumatic events. The DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual) classifies mental disorders. Causes of disorders involve biological factors (genetics, brain chemistry), psychological factors (trauma, learned patterns), and social factors (stress, environment). Stigma surrounding mental illness can prevent people from seeking help.

## Therapeutic Approaches

Psychotherapy involves treating mental disorders through psychological methods. Psychodynamic therapy explores unconscious conflicts and past experiences. Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) changes negative thought patterns and behaviors. Humanistic therapy emphasizes self-exploration and personal growth. Behavioral therapy modifies behaviors through conditioning techniques. Family therapy addresses family dynamics affecting individuals. Group therapy provides peer support. Biomedical therapies include medications (antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers) and procedures (ECT, TMS). Evidence-based practice integrates research evidence, clinical expertise, and patient values. Prevention and early intervention improve outcomes. Self-care practices include exercise, sleep, nutrition, mindfulness, and social connection.

## Neuroscience and Behavior

Neuroscience examines the nervous system's role in behavior. Neurons are nerve cells transmitting information through electrical and chemical signals. Neurotransmitters are chemicals transmitting signals between neurons (dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, GABA, glutamate). The central nervous system includes brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system connects CNS to body. The brain's cerebrum handles higher functions; cerebellum coordinates movement; brainstem controls vital functions. The limbic system processes emotions and memory (amygdala, hippocampus, hypothalamus). Brain plasticity allows neural connections to change with experience. Neuroimaging techniques (MRI, fMRI, PET scans) visualize brain structure and activity. Hormones from the endocrine system also influence behavior and emotions.

## Motivation and Emotion

Motivation drives behavior toward goals. Intrinsic motivation comes from internal satisfaction; extrinsic motivation from external rewards. Maslow's hierarchy proposes needs from physiological to self-actualization. Drive-reduction theory suggests behavior aims to reduce internal tension. Instinct theory proposes innate behavioral patterns. Emotion involves physiological arousal, expressive behaviors, and conscious experience. Major emotion theories include James-Lange (emotion follows physiological response), Cannon-Bard (emotion and response occur simultaneously), and Schachter-Singer (emotion depends on interpretation of arousal). Basic emotions include happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, and disgust. Emotional intelligence involves recognizing and managing emotions in oneself and others. Stress triggers the fight-or-flight response; chronic stress harms health.

## Learning and Conditioning

Learning is relatively permanent behavior change through experience. Classical conditioning (Pavlov) pairs neutral stimuli with natural responses. Operant conditioning (Skinner) uses consequences to shape behavior. Positive reinforcement adds desirable stimuli to increase behavior; negative reinforcement removes aversive stimuli to increase behavior. Punishment decreases behavior. Observational learning (Bandura) occurs by watching others. Latent learning happens without immediate reinforcement. Insight learning involves sudden problem comprehension. Habituation reduces response to repeated stimuli. Generalization responds similarly to similar stimuli; discrimination distinguishes between stimuli. Extinction weakens conditioned responses when reinforcement stops.
