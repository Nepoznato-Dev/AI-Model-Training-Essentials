# Climate Technology and Green Innovation

Climate technology encompasses the tools, systems, and innovations designed to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, adapt to climate change, and transition the global economy to a sustainable footing. It's arguably the most important technology domain of the 21st century — the difference between a manageable 1.5°C of warming and a catastrophic 3°C+ depends largely on how quickly these technologies can be deployed at scale. The challenge is not just scientific but economic, political, and social.

---

## The Carbon Budget

### Greenhouse Gas Emissions by Sector

| Sector | Share of Global Emissions | Key Sources |
|--------|--------------------------|-------------|
| **Energy (electricity and heat)** | ~25% | Coal; natural gas; oil power plants |
| **Agriculture, forestry, land use** | ~18% | Livestock (methane); deforestation; fertilisers (nitrous oxide) |
| **Industry** | ~21% | Cement; steel; chemicals; manufacturing |
| **Transport** | ~16% | Road vehicles; aviation; shipping |
| **Buildings** | ~6% | Heating; cooling; cooking |
| **Other energy** | ~10% | Fugitive emissions from fossil fuel extraction; pipelines |

### The Emissions Gap

| Concept | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Paris Agreement target** | Limit warming to 1.5°C or well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels |
| **Carbon budget (1.5°C)** | ~400 billion tonnes of CO₂ remaining (from 2023); at current rates, exhausted by ~2030 |
| **Current trajectory** | ~2.5–2.9°C of warming by 2100 based on current policies |
| **Net zero target** | Most countries have pledged net zero by 2050 (some by 2060 or 2070) |
| **Emissions gap** | The difference between what's needed and what countries are actually doing |

## Energy Technologies

### Renewable Energy

| Technology | How It Works | Cost Trend | Limitation |
|-----------|-------------|------------|------------|
| **Solar PV** | Photovoltaic cells convert sunlight to electricity | Down ~90% since 2010; cheapest electricity in history | Intermittent; needs storage; land use |
| **Onshore wind** | Wind turns turbines connected to generators | Down ~70% since 2010 | Intermittent; visual impact; noise |
| **Offshore wind** | Wind turbines in oceans; stronger, steadier winds | Declining but more expensive than onshore | Marine ecosystems; maintenance; grid connection |
| **Hydroelectric** | Water flows through turbines | Mature technology; low cost | Limited new sites; ecosystem disruption; drought risk |
| **Geothermal** | Heat from Earth's interior | Stable; baseload | Location-specific; exploration risk |
| **Tidal / wave** | Ocean movements drive turbines | Early stage; expensive | Predictable but harsh environment; limited sites |

### Energy Storage

| Technology | Duration | Capacity | Best For |
|-----------|----------|----------|----------|
| **Lithium-ion batteries** | Hours | Medium | Grid peaking; EVs; home storage |
| **Pumped hydro** | Hours to days | Very large | Grid-scale; mature technology |
| **Flow batteries** | Hours | Scalable | Long-duration; industrial |
| **Compressed air** | Hours to days | Large | Grid-scale; needs geological formations |
| **Green hydrogen** | Days to seasons | Very large | Seasonal storage; industrial heat; shipping |
| **Thermal storage** | Hours | Medium | Concentrated solar; industrial processes |

### Nuclear Energy

| Type | Description | Status |
|------|-------------|--------|
| **Fission (current)** | Splitting heavy atoms (uranium) to release energy | ~10% of global electricity; low carbon; controversial |
| **Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)** | Factory-built; smaller; potentially safer | In development; first deployments expected late 2020s |
| **Fusion** | Fusing light atoms (hydrogen) to release energy | Experimental (ITER); decades away from commercial |
| **Generation IV reactors** | Advanced designs; better fuel use; less waste | Research stage; some prototypes |

## Transport Decarbonisation

### Electric Vehicles

| Aspect | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Battery EVs (BEVs)** | Fully electric; zero tailpipe emissions; dominant technology |
| **Plug-in hybrids (PHEVs)** | Electric + combustion; can run on either; bridge technology |
| **Range** | 300–500 km typical; improving with each generation |
| **Charging** | Home (slow); public DC fast chargers (20-30 min to 80%) |
| **Grid impact** | Smart charging; vehicle-to-grid; managed charging reduces grid stress |
| **Cost** | Purchase price approaching parity with ICE vehicles; total cost of ownership already lower |

### Hard-to-Electrify Transport

| Sector | Challenge | Solution |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Aviation** | Batteries too heavy for long flights | Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF); hydrogen; efficiency improvements |
| **Shipping** | Massive energy requirements | Green ammonia; methanol; wind-assist; slow steaming |
| **Heavy trucking** | Long range; high payload | Hydrogen fuel cells; electric highways; larger batteries |
| **Rail** | Already mostly electrified in many countries | Continue electrification; hydrogen for non-electrified lines |

## Industrial Decarbonisation

### Key Challenges

| Industry | Emissions Source | Decarbonisation Pathway |
|----------|-----------------|------------------------|
| **Cement** | Chemical process (calcination) + energy | Alternative binders; carbon capture; green hydrogen kilns |
| **Steel** | Coal used as reducing agent | Green hydrogen direct reduced iron (DRI); electric arc furnaces |
| **Chemicals** | Feedstock + energy | Green hydrogen; bio-based feedstocks; electrification |
| **Plastics** | Fossil feedstock | Bio-plastics; chemical recycling; circular economy |
| **Aluminium** | Electricity-intensive smelting | Renewable-powered smelters; inert anode technology |

### Carbon Capture, Utilisation, and Storage (CCUS)

| Technology | Description | Status |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| **Point-source capture** | Capture CO₂ from industrial flues before it enters the atmosphere | Pilot/demonstration stage; expensive |
| **Direct Air Capture (DAC)** | Remove CO₂ directly from ambient air | Early commercial (Climeworks, Carbon Engineering); very expensive |
| **Carbon utilisation** | Convert captured CO₂ into products (fuels, concrete, chemicals) | Research stage for most pathways |
| **Geological storage** | Inject CO₂ into deep rock formations | Proven technology; limited deployment; monitoring required |
| **Bioenergy with CCS (BECCS)** | Burn biomass; capture CO₂; results in net-negative emissions | Theoretical; limited deployment |

## Carbon Removal

| Approach | Description | Scale Potential | Cost |
|----------|-------------|----------------|------|
| **Reforestation and afforestation** | Plant trees; restore forests | High | Low ($10-50/tonne) |
| **Soil carbon sequestration** | Regenerative agriculture; cover crops; no-till | High | Low to negative (saves money) |
| **Biochar** | Pyrolyse biomass; add to soil | Medium | Medium ($50-200/tonne) |
| **Enhanced weathering** | Spread crushed rock that absorbs CO₂ | High | Medium ($50-200/tonne) |
| **Ocean-based approaches** | Ocean fertilisation; alkalinity enhancement | Uncertain | Unknown |
| **DAC + storage** | Direct air capture with geological storage | High | High ($250-600/tonne currently; expected to fall) |

## Climate Adaptation Technology

| Domain | Technology | Description |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Agriculture** | Drought-resistant crops | Genetically modified or bred for water efficiency |
| **Agriculture** | Precision irrigation | Sensors + AI optimise water use |
| **Water** | Desalination | Remove salt from seawater; energy-intensive but improving |
| **Water** | Flood management | Smart levees; sponge cities; early warning systems |
| **Infrastructure** | Heat-resistant materials | Roads; railways; buildings that withstand higher temperatures |
| **Early warning** | Weather prediction AI | Better forecasting of extreme events |
| **Insurance** | Climate risk modelling | Pricing risk; informing land-use decisions |
| **Migration** | Planned relocation | Managing climate displacement |

## The Economics of Climate Action

| Concept | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Carbon pricing** | Tax or cap-and-trade systems that put a price on emissions |
| **Green premiums** | The extra cost of zero-carbon alternatives vs fossil fuel equivalents |
| **Learning curves** | Costs of clean technologies fall with deployment (Wright's Law) |
| **Stranded assets** | Fossil fuel reserves and infrastructure that become worthless in a net-zero world |
| **Just transition** | Ensuring workers and communities dependent on fossil fuels aren't left behind |
| **Climate finance** | Funding for mitigation and adaptation, especially in developing countries ($100B/year pledge) |

## Summary

Climate technology spans the entire economy — energy, transport, industry, agriculture, and carbon removal. Solar and wind are now the cheapest sources of new electricity in most of the world, but intermittency requires storage (batteries, pumped hydro, green hydrogen) and grid modernisation. Electric vehicles are replacing combustion engines for passenger cars, but aviation, shipping, and heavy industry need different solutions: hydrogen, sustainable fuels, and carbon capture. Industrial decarbonisation (cement, steel, chemicals) is the hardest challenge because emissions are embedded in chemical processes. Carbon removal — from reforestation to direct air capture — is necessary to address residual emissions. Adaptation technology helps societies cope with changes already locked in by past emissions. The economics are shifting: clean technologies follow learning curves, costs fall with deployment, and the cost of inaction vastly exceeds the cost of action. The question is no longer whether the transition will happen, but whether it will happen fast enough.
