# Critical Thinking

## Introduction

Critical thinking is the disciplined process of actively and skillfully conceptualizing, applying, analyzing, synthesizing, and evaluating information. This guide provides frameworks and techniques to improve critical thinking abilities.

## Core Components

### The Elements of Thought (Paul-Elder Framework)

| Element | Questions to Ask |
|---------|------------------|
| Purpose | What am I trying to accomplish? |
| Question | What question am I addressing? |
| Information | What data/evidence am I using? |
| Inferences | What conclusions am I drawing? |
| Concepts | What ideas am I using? |
| Assumptions | What am I taking for granted? |
| Implications | What are the consequences? |
| Point of View | From what perspective? |

### Intellectual Standards

Apply these standards to your thinking:
- **Clarity**: Can you elaborate? Can you give an example?
- **Accuracy**: Is it true? How can we verify?
- **Precision**: Can you be more specific?
- **Relevance**: How does that relate to the issue?
- **Depth**: What complexities are involved?
- **Breadth**: Do we need to consider another point of view?
- **Logic**: Does this follow from the evidence?
- **Significance**: Is this the most important factor?
- **Fairness**: Am I being biased?

## Critical Thinking Frameworks

### RED Model

**R**ecognize assumptions
- Identify what you're assuming
- Question underlying beliefs
- Notice when others make assumptions

**E**valuate arguments
- Analyze evidence quality
- Check logical consistency
- Consider alternative explanations

**D**raw conclusions
- Weigh all evidence
- Avoid overgeneralization
- Acknowledge uncertainty

### Bloom's Taxonomy (Cognitive Domain)

```
        Create
         /\
        /  \
       /    \
   Evaluate  \
     /        \
    /          \
 Analyze ------ Apply
   /            \
  /              \
 Understand ----- Remember
```

Progress through levels:
1. **Remember**: Recall facts and concepts
2. **Understand**: Explain ideas or concepts
3. **Apply**: Use information in new situations
4. **Analyze**: Draw connections among ideas
5. **Evaluate**: Justify a stand or decision
6. **Create**: Produce new or original work

### Socratic Questioning

Six types of Socratic questions:

1. **Clarification**: "What do you mean by...?"
2. **Probing assumptions**: "What are you assuming?"
3. **Probing reasons/evidence**: "Why do you think that is true?"
4. **Viewpoints/perspectives**: "What would someone who disagrees say?"
5. **Probing implications**: "What are the consequences?"
6. **Question the question**: "Why is this question important?"

## Logical Reasoning

### Deductive Reasoning

From general premises to specific conclusion:

```
Premise 1: All humans are mortal
Premise 2: Socrates is human
Conclusion: Therefore, Socrates is mortal
```

**Valid forms**:
- Modus Ponens: If P then Q; P; therefore Q
- Modus Tollens: If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P
- Hypothetical Syllogism: If P then Q; if Q then R; therefore if P then R

### Inductive Reasoning

From specific observations to general conclusion:

```
Observation 1: Swan 1 is white
Observation 2: Swan 2 is white
...
Observation N: Swan N is white
Conclusion: All swans are white
```

Note: Conclusions are probable, not certain.

### Abductive Reasoning

Inference to the best explanation:

```
Observation: The grass is wet
Possible explanations: Rain, sprinkler, dew
Best explanation: It rained (given context)
```

Common in diagnosis and troubleshooting.

## Argument Analysis

### Identifying Arguments

An argument consists of:
- **Premises**: Supporting statements
- **Conclusion**: Claim being supported

**Indicator words**:
- Premises: because, since, given that, for
- Conclusion: therefore, thus, hence, so, consequently

### Evaluating Arguments

**Check validity**: Does the conclusion follow from premises?
**Check soundness**: Are the premises actually true?
**Check relevance**: Do premises support the conclusion?
**Check sufficiency**: Is there enough evidence?

### Common Logical Fallacies

| Fallacy | Description | Example |
|---------|-------------|---------|
| Ad Hominem | Attacking the person, not argument | "You're wrong because you're young" |
| Straw Man | Misrepresenting opponent's position | "You want regulation? So you want communism!" |
| False Dilemma | Presenting only two options | "Either you're with us or against us" |
| Appeal to Authority | Citing irrelevant authority | "Celebrity X says this medicine works" |
| Slippery Slope | Unwarranted chain reaction | "If we allow A, soon Z will happen" |
| Circular Reasoning | Conclusion in premises | "The Bible is true because it says so" |
| Post Hoc | Assuming causation from correlation | "I wore red and won; red brings luck" |
| Hasty Generalization | Small sample size | "Met two rude people from city X; all are rude" |

## Problem Decomposition

### First Principles Thinking

Break down complex problems into fundamental truths:

1. Identify and define current assumptions
2. Break down the problem into fundamental principles
3. Create new solutions from scratch

**Example**: SpaceX rocket costs
- Assumption: Rockets are expensive
- First principle: What are rockets made of?
- Materials cost: 2% of typical rocket price
- Solution: Build rockets in-house

### MECE Principle

**M**utually **E**xclusive, **C**ollectively **E**xhaustive

Break problems into parts that:
- Don't overlap (mutually exclusive)
- Cover everything (collectively exhaustive)

**Example**: Revenue breakdown
- Revenue = Price × Quantity
- Quantity = New customers + Returning customers
- Mutually exclusive and covers all revenue sources

### Issue Trees

Visual decomposition of problems:

```
How to increase profit?
├── Increase Revenue
│   ├── Increase Price
│   └── Increase Quantity
│       ├── New Customers
│       └── More per Customer
└── Decrease Costs
    ├── Fixed Costs
    └── Variable Costs
```

## Decision Making Models

### Rational Decision Making Model

1. Define the problem
2. Identify decision criteria
3. Weight the criteria
4. Generate alternatives
5. Evaluate alternatives
6. Select the best option

### OODA Loop

**O**bserve → **O**rient → **D**ecide → **A**ct

Iterative cycle for rapid decision making:
- **Observe**: Gather information
- **Orient**: Analyze and synthesize
- **Decide**: Choose course of action
- **Act**: Implement decision

### Cynefin Framework

Classify problems by complexity:

| Domain | Characteristics | Approach |
|--------|----------------|----------|
| Clear | Cause-effect obvious | Sense-Categorize-Respond |
| Complicated | Cause-effect analyzable | Sense-Analyze-Respond |
| Complex | Cause-effect unclear | Probe-Sense-Respond |
| Chaotic | No clear cause-effect | Act-Sense-Respond |
| Confused | Unclear which domain | Assess to categorize |

### Decision Matrix

| Criteria | Weight | Option A | Option B | Option C |
|----------|--------|----------|----------|----------|
| Cost | 0.3 | 8 | 6 | 9 |
| Quality | 0.4 | 7 | 9 | 6 |
| Time | 0.3 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| **Score** | | **7.0** | **7.5** | **7.5** |

Score = Σ(weight × rating)

## Cognitive Biases to Watch For

### Confirmation Bias
Tendency to seek/interpret information confirming existing beliefs.

**Mitigation**: Actively seek disconfirming evidence.

### Anchoring Bias
Over-relying on first piece of information encountered.

**Mitigation**: Consider multiple reference points.

### Availability Heuristic
Overestimating importance of easily recalled information.

**Mitigation**: Seek statistical data, not just anecdotes.

### Sunk Cost Fallacy
Continuing based on past investment rather than future value.

**Mitigation**: Ask "Would I start this today?"

### Overconfidence Bias
Overestimating knowledge or predictive ability.

**Mitigation**: Seek feedback, consider base rates.

## Summary

Critical thinking skills include:
- Recognizing and questioning assumptions
- Evaluating evidence and arguments
- Identifying logical fallacies
- Decomposing complex problems
- Applying structured decision frameworks
- Being aware of cognitive biases
